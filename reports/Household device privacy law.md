# Law follows who runs the vault

## What this project can claim, and what it must not

**Must not claim**

- **"HIPAA compliant," "HIPAA certified," or "HHS certified."** HHS says it does not certify products as "HIPAA compliant." A household device that stores a family's own records is, on these facts, outside covered-entity and business-associate status. A seal of that kind is the claim the FTC pleaded against GoodRx. Safer: "This project is not a HIPAA product and is not offered as a HIPAA business associate. It is designed so a consumer assistant receives a checked answer rather than the raw record. That design is not a HIPAA certification."
- **"Zero data exposure" or "nobody can see your data."** Those sentences say disclosure is impossible. They cover the TEE operator, the host, logs, a bug, legal process, and backup metadata, none of which this research established as unreachable. NEAR AI Cloud's attestation, logging, and key handling were **not verified**. Safer: "In the ordinary design, the assistant receives only a checked answer. The model that handles the records is designed to run in a TEE operated by NEAR AI. This is not a promise that disclosure is impossible. We do not receive your records in the ordinary design. People who operate the TEE provider, and anyone who compromises the device or the backup, are outside that sentence."
- **"ChatGPT and Claude never see sensitive data, so their training policies do not matter."** Their consumer terms attach to whatever those services actually receive. A checked answer that still contains an SSN or a diagnosis is Content or an Input. Consumer plans are not an enterprise business-associate agreement and are not zero-data retention. Safer: "The design intent is that ChatGPT and Claude receive a filtered answer, not the file. If sensitive text is included in what they receive, their consumer terms allow training unless the user has opted out, and Anthropic's opt-out still has a safety-review exception. This project does not turn a consumer account into an enterprise BAA."
- **"End-to-end encrypted" or "Google cannot see the backup,"** when the true fact is only that the device encrypts before upload. Google's "Google cannot decrypt your files" sentence is about Workspace client-side encryption, a different product. Consumer Drive metadata practices were **not verified**. Safer: "Files are encrypted on the device before they are uploaded. Google still has the account and whatever metadata Drive stores. This is not Google's Workspace client-side encryption product, and it is not a promise that metadata exposure is impossible."
- **"IRS safeguarded" or "Publication 4557 compliant."** Publication 4557 is education for tax professionals. Publication 1075 is for government agencies that receive federal tax information. A family storing its own return is not a preparer. Safer: "A household copy of your own return is not a tax-preparer engagement. If this were later offered to paid preparers as software that holds or files client returns, the FTC Safeguards Rule and IRC 7216 would have to be analyzed separately. This README is not tax advice."
- **"Parents can access all of a teen's medical records,"** or any line that the device includes confidential reproductive, STI, mental-health, or substance-use records a clinic is required to withhold. Safer: "The device stores and answers questions about records a person has placed on it. It does not obtain records a clinic or insurer is required to withhold."
- **"COPPA does not apply because a parent owns the device,"** if a commercial version lets children under 13 use it. Safer, for the hackathon device as described: "This private family installation is not being offered as a commercial online service." For a sold product: "COPPA is about collection from children under 13 by a commercial operator. Ages 13 through 17 are outside that line and can still be inside state minor-privacy laws."
- **"The AI gives medical advice" or "the AI gives tax advice,"** or any line that the output is safe to act on. Safer: "Answers are drafts for the family to read. They are not medical, legal, or tax advice."

**Can claim, because the sentence stays inside the design**

- The assistant is designed to receive only an answer the device has checked.
- The model that sees the stored records is designed to run inside a TEE provided by NEAR AI.
- In the ordinary design, the maker does not receive the family's records.
- Files intended for backup are encrypted on the device before upload, with the key kept by the family.
- The project is a household tool and a hackathon build. It is not a HIPAA program, not tax advice, and not a complete copy of a clinic's chart.

## A family vault stays outside HIPAA until a clinic is the customer

**HIPAA applies to a health plan, a health care clearinghouse, or a health care provider that transmits health information electronically in connection with a standard transaction**, and, where the rules say so, to a business associate of one of those. That is the statute at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1(a) and the definition in 45 C.F.R. § 160.103 ([42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d-1); [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103)). HHS states the consequence in one sentence: if an entity is neither a covered entity nor a business associate, it does not have to comply with the HIPAA Rules ([HHS, Covered Entities and Business Associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/index.html)). Holding medical files is not the test. A health care provider, in the regulation, is a person who furnishes, bills, or is paid for health care in the normal course of business. A transaction is a claims, eligibility, enrollment, or similar transmission between two parties.

On the stated facts, **the family is unlikely a covered entity**. Operating a box that holds copies of clinic records, a symptom diary, tax forms, and passports is not furnishing care, running a plan, or converting claims into standard transactions. **The maker is unlikely a covered entity** for the same reason, under the working assumption that it does not provide care, insurance, or billing. HITECH did not add families, app makers, or personal-health-record vendors to the covered-entity list. It made business associates directly liable and sent a separate breach duty for personal-health-record vendors to the FTC ([HHS business-associate page](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html); Omnibus Rule, [78 Fed. Reg. 5566 (Jan. 25, 2013)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)).

Protected health information is a legal category, not a synonym for "medical record." HHS's cloud guidance says individually identifiable health information is called PHI when a covered entity or business associate creates, receives, maintains, or transmits it ([HHS cloud guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html), content last reviewed December 23, 2022). OCR's February 2016 app scenarios, still the document HHS points developers to, say a developer that only offers services to consumers, including a consumer who types her own readings or uploads a portal download herself, is not likely a covered entity or a business associate ([OCR health-app scenarios](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-health-app-developer-scenarios-2-2016.pdf)). The 2013 preamble says a personal-health-record vendor is subject to HIPAA as a business associate only for records offered to individuals on behalf of covered entities ([78 Fed. Reg. 5566](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)). A teenager's records do not flip that status. HIPAA's parent-and-minor rule, 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(g), binds covered entities. It never starts if no covered entity holds the record.

**Business-associate status depends on whose behalf the vendor acts.** The regulatory definition is a person who, other than as workforce, creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI for a covered entity, or provides listed services that involve disclosure of PHI ([45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103)). Status follows the definition, not the paperwork: the Omnibus preamble says a person becomes a business associate by meeting the definition, whether or not a contract is signed. HHS's software-vendor FAQ, last reviewed December 28, 2022, says merely selling software is not enough if the vendor never has access, and that hosting or troubleshooting access is enough ([HHS software-vendor FAQ](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/is-software-vendor-business-associate/index.html)).

Four postures follow. Software the family runs, with the maker having no access, is unlikely to make the maker a business associate, because there is no covered entity in the chain. A vendor that only sells to consumers is in the same place. A personal-health-record product offered to individuals, and not on behalf of a covered entity, is unlikely a business associate. **A vendor that hosts or can access electronic PHI for a covered entity is likely a business associate even if it cannot read the plaintext.** OCR's cloud guidance says lacking the encryption key does not exempt a cloud provider, and it calls these "no-view services." The conduit exception is narrow: couriers and their electronic equivalents that only transmit, plus temporary storage incident to transmission. Persistent storage is not a conduit, even if the vendor never looks. No HHS page reviewed uses the words "trusted execution environment." The no-view guidance is the closest official analogue. Processing a prompt to produce an answer is a service. Whether purely ephemeral inference, with no persistence and no key, could still be a conduit was **not decided** in the OCR materials reviewed; the cited text points toward business-associate status whenever a covered entity is the customer and the cloud processes or stores the data.

Applied here: while the customer is the family, Google Drive and NEAR AI Cloud are unlikely HIPAA business associates. If a covered entity later used the same backup or the same model for electronic PHI, Google's persistent encrypted storage is likely a business associate under the no-view guidance, and NEAR is likely a business associate or subcontractor if it processes that PHI for the covered entity, including inside a TEE. ChatGPT or Claude, on the stated design, sees a checked answer. For the family, HIPAA does not attach even if an assistant saw the records. For a later covered-entity deployment, an assistant that receives electronic PHI to perform a function for the covered entity is the fact pattern in HHS's October 21, 2026 business-associate FAQ, which uses a chatbot on a provider portal as an example ([HHS business-associate FAQ](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html)). An assistant that receives only a non-PHI checked answer, or that the individual uses on the individual's own behalf, matches the 2016 consumer scenarios.

No 2024, 2025, or 2026 final rule was found that adds a household or a direct-to-consumer maker to the covered-entity list. Two real developments do not do that work. On June 18, 2025, the Northern District of Texas in *Carmen Purl v. HHS* vacated most of the 2024 reproductive-health Privacy Rule; HHS's fact sheet is the primary account, and it says the court left some Notice of Privacy Practices amendments in place with a February 16, 2026 compliance date ([HHS reproductive-health fact sheet](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/reproductive-health/final-rule-fact-sheet/index.html)). A secondary source reports the Fifth Circuit dismissed the remaining appeal on September 10, 2025 ([Health Law Diagnosis](https://www.healthlawdiagnosis.com/2025/09/appeals-dropped-of-decision-vacating-hipaa-reproductive-health-privacy-rule-confirming-apparent-end-of-the-rule-and-attestation-requirement/)). The eCFR text retrieved November 8, 2026 still contained reproductive-health attestation language. That conflict is recorded and unresolved; either way, the 2024 rule restricted uses by entities that were already covered. It did not create a new covered-entity category. Separately, a Security Rule update was proposed on January 6, 2025, and it would revise duties of entities that are already regulated ([90 Fed. Reg. 898](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/06/2024-30983/hipaa-security-rule-to-strengthen-the-cybersecurity-of-electronic-protected-health-information)). Secondary reports say the Fall 2026 Unified Agenda moved final action to July 2027. That date was **not verified** on reginfo.gov. The proposal's summary does not expand who is covered.

The Security Rule and the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule regulate covered entities and business associates with respect to PHI ([45 C.F.R. § 164.302 and §§ 164.400–164.414](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164)). While this product stays a family-operated tool, those rules do not require a risk analysis, a business-associate agreement with Google, NEAR, OpenAI, or Anthropic, a notice of privacy practices, or a breach notice to HHS. The fact that flips the maker, and can flip NEAR or Google, is a covered entity in the relationship: a clinic that already transmits standard transactions hires the maker or deploys the device so that patient information is handled for the clinic, or a covered group health plan does the same for members. A clinic that only recommends the device, with the family in control of any link, is the 2016 "no" scenario. The clinic remains a covered entity for records the clinic itself still holds. Employer deployment depends on whether a covered group health plan is offering the tool and receiving the records. A group health plan counts only if it has 50 or more participants or is administered by an entity other than the employer ([42 U.S.C. § 1320d](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d)).

## The 2024 breach rule turns on a second source and a vendor

The Health Breach Notification Rule, 16 CFR Part 318, is the other regime. It implements 42 U.S.C. § 17937. It was issued in 2009 and substantively amended once, by a final rule published May 30, 2024, effective July 29, 2024 ([89 Fed. Reg. 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule); [eCFR Part 318](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318), fetched November 8, 2026). The eCFR source note ends at that amendment. No later amendment of the regulatory text, and no court decision vacating the 2024 rule, was found. On September 9, 2026 the FTC withdrew its 2021 policy statement. Hunton and the American Hospital Association describe that withdrawal as housekeeping, because the 2024 rule had already addressed health apps and unauthorized disclosures ([Hunton, September 16, 2026](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-rescinds-2021-policy-statement-on-health-app-data-breaches); [AHA, September 10, 2026](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-09-10-ftc-rescinds-policy-statement-extending-breach-notification-rule-apps-devices-collect-health)). Epstein Becker reads the same rescission as ending health-app coverage and unauthorized-disclosure breaches ([Epstein Becker](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/ftc-reverses-course-on-health-app-privacy)). **The codified rule is the primary source, and it still contains the 2024 definitions.** The FTC's own September 9, 2026 press release was not opened, so the agency's exact words are **not verified** here. Until that document is in hand, Epstein's "no longer apply" reading should not be used as the statement of current law.

**The rule applies to vendors of personal health records, PHR related entities, and third party service providers that maintain information of U.S. citizens or residents.** It does not apply to HIPAA-covered entities, or to any other entity to the extent it acts as a business associate ([16 CFR § 318.1(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318)). A personal health record is an electronic record of PHR identifiable health information that has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources and is managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual. The 2024 amendment changed "can be drawn" to "has the technical capacity to draw." The Commission said either the app has the technical means, such as an API, or it does not. Unused, unpublicized, and beta integrations count. Capacity that would exist only after code the developer has not written does not. At least one source must supply PHR identifiable health information. A second source can be non-health data. The record must still be managed primarily for the individual ([89 Fed. Reg. 47036–47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule)).

The preamble's own examples decide this product. A symptom tracker whose only source is the consumer is not a personal health record. The same tracker plus a geolocation API, a data broker, a wearable sync, or a calendar API is one, even if some users never connect the second source. **A build whose only source is the family loading records, with no implemented integration, matches the example of something that is not a personal health record.** A user-uploaded hospital PDF is still the consumer as the source, even though a provider created the content. An implemented Drive API that pulls files in is a second source under the calendar-API analogy, including if the family never enables it. A manual flow in which the family downloads a file and loads it is still the consumer. Client-side-encrypted export to Drive is an outbound backup, not a source the record is drawn from. An AI model that only answers questions about records already stored is a processor in the FTC's examples, not a second source. It would be a closer and unresolved question if the product wrote model-generated health facts back into the record as a distinct feed. No FTC example addresses a local open-source appliance, a PDF parser, or a model. Whether a storage vault that answers questions is itself a "covered health care provider" under the rule's catch-all is fact-intensive. The Commission said the offering must relate more than tangentially to health, which a family health-record store does, and that multiple-source capacity is still required.

**The maker is a vendor only if it offers or maintains a personal health record.** The preamble glosses that as being in the business of selling, marketing, providing, or promoting a health-related product, more than tangentially related to health ([89 Fed. Reg. 47035](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule)). The hackathon maker ships software the family runs and does not operate a store of unencrypted health records. Combined with the consumer-only-source example, coverage is unlikely. The word "providing" in the preamble is the residual uncertainty, and no opinion was found on an open-source publisher that never possesses the data. A future product the company sells or hosts is much closer to a vendor, and hosting is "maintains." Paid status alone does not create a personal health record. A paid local vault whose only source is the user, which the company never maintains, is still the consumer-only example.

If the rule applies, a vendor or PHR related entity notifies each affected U.S. individual, the FTC, and, when 500 or more residents of a state or jurisdiction are affected, prominent media in that state. Individual notice, media notice, and a service provider's notice to the vendor are due without unreasonable delay and within 60 calendar days of discovery. FTC notice for 500 or more individuals is contemporaneous with individual notice. For fewer than 500, the entity may keep a log and submit it annually, no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar year ([16 CFR §§ 318.3–318.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318)). Notice content includes what happened, the types of unsecured information (the examples include Social Security number, diagnosis, and lab results), steps people should take, what the entity is doing, and two or more contact methods. A breach includes an unauthorized disclosure, not only a hack. The preamble says a disclosure inconsistent with the company's representations, or a voluntary disclosure the consumer did not authorize, can qualify. Buried privacy-policy text is not meaningful choice. A checked answer shown to an assistant the family chose to query fits the 2009 commentary's treatment of processing the person asked for, when it matches what the family was told. A disclosure of the underlying record, or of health details the project said would not leave the device, is the GoodRx theory, and that theory applies only after a covered personal health record and a covered vendor exist. An incident confined to passports or tax forms is not, by itself, a breach under this rule. The rule has no special notice path for a teenager. Notice runs to the individual whose information was acquired.

**A violation is treated as a violation of a rule under section 18 of the FTC Act, so civil penalties are available** ([16 CFR § 318.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318)). The inflation-adjusted maximum for a knowing violation under FTC Act section 5(m)(1)(A) is **$53,088**, effective January 17, 2025, and the FTC left the 2025 levels unchanged for 2026 ([90 Fed. Reg. adjustment, January 17, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/17/2025-01361/adjustments-to-civil-penalty-amounts); [September 15, 2026 notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/15/2026-18853/civil-penalty-inflation-adjustments)). What counts as one violation was not resolved. The Commission announced GoodRx on February 1, 2023, as its first HBNR action, with a $1.5 million civil penalty, and Easy Healthcare's Premom app on May 17, 2023, with a $100,000 HBNR penalty ([WilmerHale on GoodRx](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230203-ftc-brings-first-of-its-kind-enforcement-action-against-goodrx-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule); [WilmerHale on Easy Healthcare](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230525-ftc-brings-second-enforcement-action-against-healthcare-company-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule)). BetterHelp's $7.8 million was consumer redress under section 5, not an HBNR civil penalty ([Proskauer](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2023/03/articles/data-privacy-laws/ftcs-one-two-punch-on-data-tracking-and-health-privacy/)). No later HBNR complaint was found. That is not a complete docket review. Case filings themselves were not opened; the penalty descriptions rest on the 2024 preamble and firm accounts, except that the GoodRx complaint was fetched and is the primary source for the HIPAA-seal theory discussed below.

NEAR is a third party service provider, with a duty to notify the vendor rather than the family, only if a vendor exists and NEAR holds or uses unsecured PHR identifiable health information. Access in the course of providing services does not make the service provider a PHR related entity. Whether plaintext inside a TEE is "unsecured" is unresolved. The safe harbor is the encryption method HHS specified under 42 U.S.C. § 17932(h)(2). The current text of that HHS guidance was **not re-fetched**, and no FTC or HHS source reviewed decides that a TEE meets it. Google, holding only client-side ciphertext, is outside the "unsecured" definition if the encryption is the HHS-specified method. That conclusion depends on the method actually meeting the guidance. Google is not the vendor of this record.

## Three states reach a health product before it has revenue

The family is the data subject in these statutes. Washington defines a consumer as a Washington resident, or a person whose consumer health data is collected in Washington, acting in an individual or household context ([RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010)). A regulated entity is a legal entity that conducts business in Washington or targets products to Washington consumers and that determines the purpose and means of collecting, processing, sharing, or selling consumer health data. **No household-activities exemption appeared in the exemptions section read.** The family falls outside because it is not that legal entity. "Small business" is still regulated. It is a size category that had a later start date, June 30, 2024, and the definition uses consumer counts and a revenue share, not an employee count ([Washington AG FAQ](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy)). The AG says a person that only stores data in Washington is not a regulated entity. An out-of-state open-source project is unlikely to be pulled in because the code could be downloaded in Washington. It is pulled in if it provides a product to Washington consumers and itself decides why their consumer health data is processed. A future product offered to Washington households is the likely case, including a tiny seller. A violation is a per se Consumer Protection Act violation, enforceable by the Attorney General and by private action ([RCW 19.373.090](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.090)). The private-remedy details, including a $25,000 treble-damages cap, come from a June 2023 secondary chart of the Consumer Protection Act and were not re-read from that statute. No published merits decision interpreting the Act was found. The Amazon SDK litigation was filed and consolidated; a secondary October 2026 article's account of a voluntary dismissal was **not confirmed** from the docket. No 2025 or 2026 amendment appeared in the RCW text retrieved. **Not verified** past those pages. The geofence ban, which applies to any person, forbids fencing an in-person health-care facility to identify patients or send health ads. This device does not do that, and the ban is not the jurisdictional test.

Nevada is narrower at the definition of consumer and similar in reach for a seller. A consumer is a natural person who has requested a product or service from a regulated entity and who resides in Nevada or whose consumer health data is collected in Nevada ([NRS 603A.400–.550](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html)). **Health records a family stores for itself, without requesting that storage from a Nevada business, sit outside that definition.** There is no small-business threshold in the sections read. The law took effect March 31, 2024. Sale of consumer health data requires a written authorization that expires after one year. The geofence radius is 1,750 feet around in-person care. A violation is a deceptive trade practice, and the health-data provisions do not create a private right of action ([Bass, Berry & Sims, March 20, 2024](https://bassberry.com/news/nevada-consumer-health-data-law-takes-effect-on-march-31-2024/)). A future product offered to Nevada consumers, where the seller decides purposes and means, is likely in scope from the first such household. Publishing code, without offering a product and without processing Nevadan health data, is unlikely to qualify. **Not verified** past the NRS page retrieved November 8, 2026. Civil penalty amounts under the deceptive-trade chapter were not re-read.

Connecticut runs two overlapping regimes. Since October 1, 2023, the consumer-health provisions in C.G.S. § 42-526, and the Act's provisions concerning consumer health data controllers, apply without the ordinary consumer-count or revenue threshold to a person who conducts business in Connecticut or produces products or services targeted to residents ([2025 compilation of Chapter 743jj](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm)). **Nothing in the Act applies to a person's processing of personal data in the course of purely personal or household activities**, C.G.S. § 42-524(e)(2). Public Act 25-113, effective July 1, 2026, and Public Act 26-64, effective October 1, 2026, substitute other subsections and, on the text retrieved, do not repeal that household exclusion ([Public Act 25-113](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf); [Public Act 26-64](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf)). The family's own operation, including storing a teenager's records as household processing, is that exclusion. The exclusion does not travel with a maker that processes Connecticut consumers' data as a product.

From July 1, 2026, the general Act also applies, with no volume floor, to a person with the same geographic nexus who controls or processes consumers' sensitive data ([Public Act 25-113 § 6](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf)). Sensitive data already included health data. **Effective July 1, 2026, it also includes a government-issued identification number, including a Social Security number, a passport number, a state identification card number, or a driver's license number**, and Public Act 26-64 carries that list forward from October 1, 2026. Both of those effective dates are already past as of November 8, 2026. Processing sensitive data requires consent, and the controller may not sell sensitive data without consent. Health-data controller duties add employee confidentiality, a processor contract, a facility geofence ban, and consent to sell consumer health data. Enforcement is exclusively by the Attorney General. The mandatory cure period ended December 31, 2024. "Targeted to residents" is not defined in the sections read, and no case was found applying it to a nationwide household download. A product offered to U.S. households, including Connecticut residents who can sign up, is therefore **depends** until an enforcer or a court applies that hook. Once the hook is met, there is no revenue or employee shelter for health data, and after July 1, 2026 there is none for an SSN or a passport number either. Whether the no-threshold health rules apply to a nonprofit open-source maintainer was not settled by an Attorney General opinion. The general Act exempts nonprofits; the health-section exemption list, as compiled, does not repeat that exemption.

California is a threshold statute. A CCPA business is a for-profit legal entity that does business in California and meets one of three tests: preceding-year gross revenue over the adjusted figure, buying, selling, or sharing personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households, or deriving 50 percent or more of annual revenue from selling or sharing ([Civ. Code § 1798.140(d)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/)). **The California Privacy Protection Agency's figure, effective January 1, 2025, is $26,625,000**, and no 2026 revision was posted ([CPPA monetary thresholds](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html)). The Attorney General's FAQ still states the unadjusted "over $25 million." The Agency's CPI page is the adjustment. Sensitive personal information includes a Social Security number, a passport number, and personal information collected and analyzed concerning a consumer's health. A hackathon and a small early product are unlikely to be a business. The January 1, 2026 cybersecurity-audit and automated-decision rules sit on top of business status plus further tests, so they do not independently reach a sub-threshold maker. The audit counts cited in a law-firm FAQ were not re-quoted from the regulation text. A household question-answering device is a poor fit for the regulatory definition of a significant automated decision on the materials retrieved.

New York's SHIELD Act is the safeguard duty that does not wait for a consumer count. **Any person or business that owns or licenses computerized private information of a New York resident must implement reasonable safeguards** ([GBL § 899-bb](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-BB)). Private information includes an SSN and medical information, generally in combination with a name and subject to an encryption structure ([GBL § 899-aa](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-AA)). A small business, under 50 employees or under $3 million gross revenue in each of the last three fiscal years or under $5 million in assets, complies with a program appropriate to its size. That is a scaled duty, not an exemption. There is no private right of action. A future product whose operator owns or licenses users' SSNs or medical information is the likely case. If the household owns the data and the maker never receives it, the maker is unlikely to own or license it. A literal reading of "any person" could be argued to cover a New York family keeping its own files. The small-business examples and the Attorney General materials are written for organizations. Treat household self-storage as unlikely to be the enforcement target, and treat the statutory wording as unresolved. FindLaw marks the section current as of January 1, 2026. **Not verified** past November 8, 2026.

Maryland's Online Data Privacy Act took effect October 1, 2025, at 35,000 consumers or 10,000 plus more than 20 percent of revenue from selling personal data ([Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/resources-info/Pages/data-privacy.aspx)). It does not change the answer at hackathon scale. The Attorney General says a controller may not sell sensitive data, which includes consumer health data. A law-firm guide adds that collection of sensitive data is limited to what is strictly necessary for the product the consumer requested. **That "strictly necessary" sentence was not confirmed in the code**, which was not opened.

Breach-notification statutes are a separate, nationwide pattern. Every state has one; that proposition was not re-proven by opening all 50 texts. California requires an individual or a business that conducts business in California and owns or licenses computerized personal information to notify a resident after a breach of unencrypted personal information, within 30 calendar days on the 2025 text retrieved, and to send a sample to the Attorney General above 500 residents ([Civ. Code § 1798.82](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-3/part-4/title-1-81/section-1798-82/)). The Social Security number element was confirmed. The full medical-information sentences of the California definition were not captured, so they are not quoted here. New York's private-information definition expressly includes an SSN and medical information. A client-side-encrypted backup, with the key staying with the family and not compromised, is the fact pattern the encryption carve-outs are written for. Whether a particular backup meets each statute's definition of encrypted was not analyzed. These duties arise when a breach happens. They do not require the maker to be a health-care business. They do require covered computerized data about a resident, plus the statute's own-or-license hook.

Across the statutes that were read, employee count matters for New York's scaled program and was not a threshold in Washington, Nevada, Connecticut, California, or Maryland. Confidential computing and an encrypted Drive backup do not create an exemption in any of those laws. They bear on who decides purposes and means, whether a cloud operator is a processor that needs a contract, and whether a later breach involves readable information and a key. Washington processor duties and Nevada's processor contract run to a person processing consumer health data on behalf of a regulated entity. If the maker operates the confidential-computing path for Washington or Nevada users of a sold product, processor-versus-regulated-entity status depends on who decides purposes and means. NEAR's terms were not reviewed against those contract requirements.

## Teen privacy splits at age 13 and at the clinic door

**COPPA regulates operators of commercial websites and online services directed to children under 13, or with actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information from a child under 13.** It does not regulate teenagers ages 13 through 17. The 2025 amendments were published April 22, 2025, effective June 23, 2025, with a general compliance date of April 22, 2026, so both dates have passed ([90 Fed. Reg. 16918](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)). The current definition still says a child is an individual under 13 ([16 C.F.R. § 312.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)). Personal information now expressly includes a government-issued identifier such as a Social Security number or passport number, and a biometric identifier that can be used for recognition, including a voiceprint. Collection means gathering personal information from a child, including prompting the child, enabling the child to post it, and passively tracking the child. An operator is a person who operates a commercial website or online service. A nonprofit exempt from FTC Act coverage is excluded.

**A private parent-administered household device is unlikely to be a COPPA operator**, because the rule requires a commercial website or online service. A future product is unlikely to be covered merely because it stores a 13-to-17-year-old's records. It becomes a COPPA question if it collects personal information from a user under 13: a child using the assistant, a voice interaction that captures the child's voice, or a parent telling the service that a profile belongs to a 10-year-old, which is the fact pattern that creates actual knowledge on a general-audience service. The 2025 rule did not, in the sources reviewed, replace actual knowledge with a "should have known" standard for general-audience services. A parent typing a teen's passport number into a device the parent bought is unlikely to be collection from a child. That conclusion rests on the regulation's "from a child" verb. The FTC's longstanding COPPA FAQ URL returned HTTP 404, so an FTC FAQ sentence on parent-supplied data was **not retrieved** and is not quoted. The amendments that would matter if under-13 collection ever happens are the passport and voiceprint definitions, a written retention limit, and separate verifiable parental consent for a third-party disclosure that is not integral to the service. Those consent details are taken from the preamble and from Gibson Dunn's account of the final rule; the current text of § 312.5 was not fetched ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/)). A violation of a COPPA rule is an unfair or deceptive act or practice ([FTC amicus citing 15 U.S.C. § 6502(c)](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ShanahanAmicusBriefFiled.pdf)).

Several states regulate online services used by minors through age 17. **For a future commercial product, the New York Child Data Protection Act is the clearest verified fit.** It took effect June 20, 2025, and the Attorney General treats a connected device as a covered service ([NY AG guidance](https://ag.ny.gov/child-data-protection-act-guidance)). A covered user includes a user the operator actually knows is a minor. For users 12 and under, processing permitted by COPPA is permitted. For users 13 through 17, processing personal data requires the minor's consent unless it is strictly necessary for a listed purpose, including the specific product the covered user requested. The same guidance says a parent may agree to a product for a child, and the operator may then process data the parent provides, without a separate consent from the child, only for processing strictly necessary to provide that product. Further processing of a 13-to-17-year-old's data needs that minor's consent. A family product is probably not "primarily directed" to minors. It can still have actual knowledge if the parent creates a profile that records a 15-year-old's age. Final rules after the May 19, 2025 guidance were **not verified**.

Maryland's Age-Appropriate Design Code remained in effect in the opinions retrieved. On November 24, 2025, the district court denied a motion to dismiss and wrote that, pending litigation, the Kids Code remains in effect ([memorandum opinion](https://netchoice.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/MD-AADC_Mem.-Op.-denying-MTD.pdf)). A July 7, 2026 order allowed an amended complaint and did not enjoin the statute. An injunction after that date was **not verified**. Hunton describes coverage of online products reasonably likely to be accessed by children, with an April 1, 2026 impact-assessment deadline ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/new-lawsuit-challenges-marylands-age-appropriate-design-code-act)). The code text itself was not fetched. California's Age-Appropriate Design Code is split after the Ninth Circuit's March 12, 2026 preliminary-injunction opinion: the injunction against the statute as a whole and against age estimation was vacated, and the injunction against data-use and dark-pattern restrictions was affirmed on vagueness grounds ([Ninth Circuit opinion](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/03/12/25-2366.pdf)). That is not a final judgment. A CPPA age-estimation rule implementing the statute was **not found**, and whether AB 1949 became law was **not verified**. Connecticut's minor duties run to controllers that offer an online service to consumers they know, or willfully disregard, are under 18, and the Attorney General describes limits on targeted advertising, sale, profiling, and design features that extend use ([Connecticut AG](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/sections/privacy/the-connecticut-data-privacy-act)). Public Act 25-113 amends those provisions effective July 1, 2026. A Wiley alert says the 2026 amendments expand a protected band to ages 13 through 17 and prohibit targeted advertising and sale regardless of consent. **That "regardless of consent" sentence was not confirmed against the act text** in the minors research. The state-law research did retrieve the act, and what it confirms is the sensitive-data consent rule, a ban on selling sensitive data without consent, and COPPA-aligned consent for a known child. Florida's HB 3 and Texas's SCOPE Act are unlikely to cover this device. They address addictive social platforms and services that connect users so they can socially interact. The Eleventh Circuit on October 14, 2026 held Florida's law likely constitutional as applied to the trade groups' members ([Eleventh Circuit opinion](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/files/202511881.pdf)). The Fifth Circuit on July 24, 2026 affirmed a preliminary injunction against Texas's monitoring-and-filtering requirement as preempted by 47 U.S.C. § 230. Whether Texas's August 28, 2026 en banc petition was granted by November 8, 2026 was **not verified**. Neither opinion is a holding about a household health-record device.

**Laws that let a minor consent to certain care, and that limit parent access to those records, bind the clinic, the insurer, and other holders of the clinical record.** HHS says a parent is usually the personal representative of an unemancipated minor, and it lists three situations in which the parent is not the personal representative for the related information: the minor consents to care for which other law does not require parental consent; someone else authorized to consent does so; or the parent agrees to a confidential relationship ([HHS, Personal Representatives](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/personal-representatives/index.html), last reviewed January 5, 2024). A covered entity must permit parental access when state law requires it and must refuse access when state law prohibits it. OCR's December 3, 2025 letter tells covered entities the same three exceptions and confines the HIPAA Rules to health plans, clearinghouses, most providers, and their business associates ([OCR letter](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-letter-hipaa-privacy-rule-and-parental-access-to-minor-childrens-medical-records.pdf)). California Health and Safety Code § 123115(a) says a minor's representative is not entitled to inspect the minor's patient records when the minor has an independent inspection right, when the provider determines access would harm the relationship or the minor's safety or well-being, or when the records relate to listed minor-consent services ([§ 123115](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=123115.)). Washington RCW 70.02.130 says that if a minor may consent to health care without parental consent, only the minor may exercise the medical-records chapter's patient rights for information about that care ([RCW 70.02.130](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=70.02.130)). The age thresholds quoted for California Family Code sections and for Washington mental-health and STI consent come from secondary compilations and a legislative committee document. The operative parent-access limits in § 123115 and RCW 70.02.130 were fetched. Official current text of the New York Public Health Law minor-consent sections was **not verified**; the legislature page timed out, and the citation map is a 2022 Pediatrics article.

A parent reading a copy the parent already lawfully has, or storing that copy on a device the parent bought, is unlikely to be the person those statutes regulate. **The design risk is ingestion plus marketing.** If the product only stores what a parent already received, it sits downstream of whatever the provider released. If the product connects to a portal or clinic and is built so parents can obtain records the provider is required to withhold, it is a practical end-run around duties that still bind the provider. This report does not describe how to do that. "Parents can access all of a teen's medical records" conflicts with the California and Washington limits on what a provider may release. The June 18, 2025 vacatur of the reproductive-health Privacy Rule changes provider obligations about investigations and attestations. It does not create a parent right to minor-consent records, and it does not make a household device a covered entity. Sending a checked answer about a teen's records to ChatGPT or Claude does not convert the parent into a covered entity. It can still spread a sensitive record to another company, which is a disclosure-risk and honesty problem.

A personal Google account does not become that agreement because a user uploads a file to consumer Drive. HHS says a cloud provider that stores encrypted electronic PHI for a covered entity is still generally a business associate even without the key, and that OCR does not endorse or certify products ([HHS cloud FAQ](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/2078/which-csps-offer-hipaa-compliant-cloud-services/index.html), last reviewed February 15, 2023). That FAQ does not turn a household's own files into PHI.

Google's Drive help page says files are encrypted in transit and at rest, and that Workspace client-side encryption is an extra layer an administrator enables, under which Google cannot decrypt the files. The same page says local applications with permission may still view files, and encryption does not protect files from someone who can see the screen ([Google Drive Help](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/10519333?hl=en)). A household app that encrypts before upload is the device's encryption, not Workspace client-side encryption. The general Google privacy policy and terms were not fetched, so there is no quote here of Google's sentence on content users upload to consumer Drive. Consumer Drive's handling of filenames and previews of already-encrypted blobs was **not verified**.

**A family storing its own tax returns is not the audience of the IRS safeguard publications.** Publication 4557, PDF footer "Publication 4557 (Rev. 6-2024)," is outreach to tax professionals, transmitters, and software developers. It says the FTC's financial-institution definition includes professional tax preparers, and it tells that audience to encrypt before uploading to the cloud ([IRS Publication 4557](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4557.pdf)). Publication 1075, revised November 2021, is for federal, state, and local agencies that receive federal tax information under IRC § 6103(p)(4) ([IRS Publication 1075](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1075.pdf)). The binding hooks for preparers are the Safeguards Rule, which applies to financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction and lists tax preparation firms among the examples, and requires a written information security program ([16 CFR Part 314](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-314)), and IRC § 7216, a misdemeanor for a person engaged in the business of preparing returns, or who for compensation prepares a return for another person, who knowingly or recklessly discloses information furnished for that preparation ([26 U.S.C. § 7216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7216)). The regulation's software example is a person who develops software used to prepare or file a return and holds that service out. A person who only incidentally relates to preparation is excluded ([26 C.F.R. § 301.7216-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-1)). Storage of family copies, without holding the product out as preparation or e-file software, is outside that example on the text fetched. No IRS document was fetched that uses the words "a family storing its own return is not a preparer." That sentence is the inference from the definitions. If the product were later marketed to preparers as a place to hold client returns, or held out as software used to prepare or file returns, the preparer customer would be a financial institution under the Safeguards Rule, and the maker could itself fall under the auxiliary-services definition. Disclosure of client return information to a model or a backup provider would then be a § 7216 question.

The FTC's deception cases are the reason the claim list at the top is short. HHS's marketing page, last reviewed July 26, 2013 and still the page returned for this topic, says HHS and OCR do not certify any persons or products as "HIPAA compliant" ([HHS, misleading marketing claims](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/be-aware-misleading-marketing-claims/index.html)). The GoodRx complaint alleges that a seal reading "HIPAA Secure. Patient Data Protected." signaled that the company was a HIPAA-covered entity and that its practices complied, and Count V alleges that GoodRx was not a covered entity and that its practices did not comply ([GoodRx complaint](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.407839/gov.uscourts.cand.407839.1.0.pdf)). The BetterHelp press release describes a ban on sharing mental-health information for advertising after promises to keep it private, and $7.8 million in partial refunds ([FTC, March 2, 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook)). Holland & Knight's secondary account says the complaint treated a "HIPAA certified" claim as deceptive. HHS and the FTC have also told organizations that share consumer health information that considering the HIPAA Privacy Rule is not enough, and that disclosure statements must not be deceptive under the FTC Act ([HHS guidance index](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/index.html)).

The 1983 FTC Policy Statement on Deception is the Commission's articulation of the test. The historical PDF returned 404 on November 8, 2026, so its paragraphs are not quoted. The working test, attributed to that statement and flagged as not re-downloaded, is that a claim is deceptive if it is a representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead consumers acting reasonably, and the representation is material to the decision to buy or use the product. Implied claims count. Chair Khan's fetched statement says exaggerated claims about AI capabilities need concrete evidence ([Khan statement](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/remarks-of-chair-lina-m-khan-re-joint-interagency-statement-on-ai.pdf)). Several well-known FTC blog URLs, including "Keep your AI claims in check" and the September 2024 Operation AI Comply press-release URL, returned 404. Their body text is not cited. No 2025 or 2026 FTC document on model training or AI claims was successfully fetched. Absence from this research is not evidence the Commission said nothing.

A README is not immune because it is unpaid. The GoodRx complaint frames the seal as a representation in connection with advertising, distribution, or sale, including a seal on a homepage. No case was fetched that holds, in those words, that a GitHub README is an advertisement. The inference is the ordinary deception test applied to sentences that invite families to load SSNs and health records. A purely technical description of a local prototype is a weaker case. The difference is what a reasonable reader is invited to believe.

## Conclusion

The sensitive files are not what pulls this project into HIPAA, the health-breach rule, or the state health statutes. The role is. A family running its own device, and a maker that never holds other people's records, stay on the outside of those regimes on the sources reviewed through November 8, 2026. The first implemented sync, the first hosted copy, and the first clinic contract are three different doors, and only the last one is HIPAA. Washington, Nevada, and Connecticut can open on a household product with no revenue and, for health data, with no large user count, which is why a hackathon README that already speaks as if the product were a certified medical vault is the exposure that exists before any of those customers do. Absolute sentences about who can see the data fail for a simpler reason than a missed statute: the research never established that the operator, the host, the backup metadata, or a broken filter is unreachable, and the consumer terms of the assistants that might receive a checked answer still train on what they are given unless the account opts out.
