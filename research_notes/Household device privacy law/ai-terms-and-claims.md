# AI provider terms and FTC privacy claims

Orientation notes for a US household device, fetched on November 8, 2026. This is citation-backed orientation, not legal advice. Terms pages change. Where a page’s own “effective” date is still in the future as of this fetch, or a URL returned an error, that is flagged in the section. Quotes are from the fetched text. Do not treat a quote as current after the page is updated.

Product facts used only as context: an open-source Raspberry Pi stores family SSNs, tax forms, passports, and health records, including a teenager’s. Consumer assistants (ChatGPT, Claude) ask questions. A confidential-computing cloud model (NEAR AI Cloud, a TEE) produces answers, and the device checks answers before the assistant sees them. The design intent is that ChatGPT, Claude, and Google do not receive the raw records; a TEE cloud does; the assistant receives a filtered answer. Optional client-side-encrypted Google Drive backup and import. Hackathon now; possible later consumer product.

This note does not evaluate whether NEAR’s TEE actually hides data from the operator, the host, or the model. It does not describe how to evade provider policies. It describes what the policies say.

## A1. OpenAI consumer terms (ChatGPT Free, Plus, Pro)

### Takeaway

The consumer Usage Policies, Terms of Use, and privacy policy that were fetched do not offer a HIPAA business-associate agreement, and they do not say that consumer ChatGPT is zero-retention or excluded from training. They do allow OpenAI to use consumer Content to improve the services unless the user opts out, and they tell users that transmission is not fully secure. A BAA, default no-training, and zero-data-retention are described on a separate enterprise page for the API and for named business products, including ChatGPT for Healthcare. The Usage Policies page displayed on November 8, 2026 is labeled effective November 20, 2026, which is after this fetch.

### Cited Findings

- Terms of Use, published and effective January 1, 2026, apply to “your use of ChatGPT, DALL·E, and OpenAI’s other services for individuals.” The same page says: “Our Business Terms govern use of ChatGPT Enterprise, our APIs, and our other services for businesses and developers.” URL: https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/ — [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/)
- On Content, the Terms of Use say: “You are responsible for Content, including ensuring that it does not violate any applicable law or these Terms.” They also say: “We may use Content to provide, maintain, develop, and improve our Services, comply with applicable law, enforce our terms and policies, and keep our Services safe.” And: “If you do not want us to use your Content to train our models, you can opt out by following the instructions in this article. Please note that in some cases this may limit the ability of our Services to better address your specific use case.” — [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/)
- The same Terms restrict reliance on output, not the act of typing health data or an SSN: “You must not use any Output relating to a person for any purpose that could have a legal or material impact on that person, such as making credit, educational, employment, housing, insurance, legal, medical, or other important decisions about them.” Output “may not always be accurate” and is not “a substitute for professional advice.” — [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/)
- Minimum age in those Terms: “You must be at least 13 years old… If you are under 18 you must have your parent or legal guardian’s permission to use the Services.” — [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/)
- Usage Policies page, fetched November 8, 2026, is headed “Effective: November 20, 2026” and links a “Previous Version.” The changelog entry dated 2026-10-21 says the update “clarify[s] restrictions on using OpenAI models and outputs to train other models,” teen protections, high-stakes uses, and national-security approval, and that “These changes are effective November 20, 2026.” The previous version was not fetched. URL: https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/ — [OpenAI Usage Policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/)
- That Usage Policies text does not name SSNs, health records, or HIPAA, and it does not say “do not submit your own sensitive data.” It does say: “we don’t allow attempts to compromise the privacy of others, including to aggregate, monitor, profile, or distribute individuals’ private or sensitive information without their authorization.” It also disallows “automation of high-stakes actions in sensitive areas without human review,” and the list includes “financial activities and credit,” “insurance,” “legal,” “medical,” and “essential government services.” It says: “Our rules are no substitute for legal requirements, professional duties, or ethical obligations.” — [OpenAI Usage Policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/)
- The privacy policy retrieved at https://openai.com/policies/row-privacy-policy/ is labeled “Updated: February 6, 2026.” It says OpenAI collects “Personal Data that you provide in the input to our Services (‘Content’), including your prompts and other content you upload, such as files.” It says: “we may use Content you provide us to improve our Services, for example to train the models that power ChatGPT,” with a link to opt-out instructions. On security: “no Internet or email transmission is ever fully secure or error free. Therefore, you should take special care in deciding what information you provide to the Services.” It also says OpenAI is “not responsible for circumvention of any privacy settings or security measures.” Services “are not directed to, or intended for, children under 13.” — [OpenAI Privacy Policy (ROW URL)](https://openai.com/policies/row-privacy-policy/)
- That privacy policy describes disclosure to vendors and service providers, to affiliates, in a business transfer, and to government authorities or other third parties in the listed legal, safety, fraud, and terms-enforcement circumstances. Deletion is not instantaneous: content the user deletes is removed “within 30 days” unless retained longer for legal, safety, or security reasons, or already de-identified for model improvement. Temporary chats are “automatically deleted within 30 days” unless safety or legal retention applies. — [OpenAI Privacy Policy (ROW URL)](https://openai.com/policies/row-privacy-policy/)
- Enterprise privacy page, “Updated: January 8, 2026,” is a different product set. It covers “inputs and outputs from ChatGPT Business, ChatGPT Enterprise, ChatGPT for Healthcare, ChatGPT Edu, ChatGPT for Teachers and our API Platform.” It says: “We do not train our models on your data by default.” It says training sources include “data from versions of ChatGPT and other services for individuals,” and that by default data from the named business products and “the API Platform (after March 1, 2023) isn’t used for training.” — [OpenAI Enterprise Privacy](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/)
- On that same enterprise page, HIPAA and retention controls are tied to those business products, not to Free, Plus, or Pro. “ChatGPT for Healthcare is a secure workspace designed to support HIPAA compliance and built for healthcare organizations.” Separately: “Can the API Platform be used with protected health information? We are able to sign Business Associate Agreements (BAA) in support of customers’ compliance with [HIPAA].” API inputs and outputs “may” be retained “for up to 30 days,” and “You can also request zero data retention (ZDR) for eligible endpoints if you have a qualifying use-case.” Authorized employees and abuse-review contractors can still have access under the limits stated there. — [OpenAI Enterprise Privacy](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/)
- The consumer Terms of Use and the consumer Usage Policies text that were fetched do not offer a BAA, ZDR, or a statement that consumer ChatGPT is “HIPAA compliant.” — [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/); [OpenAI Usage Policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/)

### Inferences

- Sending health data or an SSN into consumer ChatGPT would be “Content” under the January 1, 2026 Terms. Those Terms allow use of that Content to develop and improve the services, including training, unless the account opts out. Opt-out is an account setting, not a promise that the prompt never exists on OpenAI systems, is never reviewed for safety, or cannot be retained for legal or security reasons.
- The design intent that ChatGPT receives only a checked answer matters. The consumer terms govern what is actually submitted as Input. They do not become the enterprise BAA or ZDR terms because a different system, upstream, used a TEE.
- The Usage Policies’ rule about other people’s sensitive information is an authorization rule. It is not a written ban, in the text fetched, on a person entering their own SSN or health information. A product that sends a teenager’s records still has to be squared with the under-18 permission rule and the under-13 rule if the account or the data subject is a minor. That is a terms fact, not a conclusion that the household product is lawful or unlawful.
- “Designed to support HIPAA compliance” on the enterprise page is OpenAI’s description of ChatGPT for Healthcare. It is not a statement that HHS has certified any OpenAI product, and it is not language that appears in the consumer terms fetched here.

### Gaps

- The Usage Policies “Previous Version” link was not fetched. Because the page’s own effective date is November 20, 2026, the text above may be the upcoming policy rather than the policy in force on November 8, 2026.
- https://openai.com/policies/privacy-policy/ was not fetched as a separate US page. The privacy quotes are from https://openai.com/policies/row-privacy-policy/, updated February 6, 2026. US consumer terms dated January 1, 2026 independently contain the training opt-out language.
- No consumer help-center article was fetched to confirm the exact opt-out clicks for Free, Plus, and Pro, or whether Temporary Chat is available on every tier.
- No page was fetched in which OpenAI says, in those words, “consumer ChatGPT does not offer a BAA.” The finding is that the consumer documents fetched do not offer one, and the enterprise page places the BAA on the API.

## A2. Anthropic consumer terms (Claude.ai)

### Takeaway

Claude consumer privacy and consumer terms, both in force on this fetch, say Anthropic may use Inputs and Outputs to train models unless the user opts out, and that opt-out still does not cover safety-review flags or material the user explicitly reports. The consumer privacy policy does not apply to Enterprise content handled under customer agreements. The Usage Policy fetched is labeled effective November 12, 2026, after this fetch. None of the consumer pages fetched offer a HIPAA BAA.

### Cited Findings

- Consumer Terms of Service, effective October 8, 2025, audience “Individual”: “Our Commercial Terms of Service govern your use of any Anthropic API key, the Anthropic Console, or any other Anthropic offerings that reference the Commercial Terms of Service. For clarity, this does not include Claude.ai or Claude Pro use for individuals or entities.” URL: https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms — [Anthropic Consumer Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms)
- Those Consumer Terms say: “We may use Materials to provide, maintain, and improve the Services and to develop other products and services, including training our models, unless you opt out of training through your account settings. Even if you opt out, we will use Materials for model training when: (1) you provide Feedback to us regarding any Materials, or (2) your Materials are flagged for safety review to improve our ability to detect harmful content, enforce our policies, or advance our safety research.” They also reserve a right “to report information from or about you, including but not limited to Inputs, Outputs, or Actions to law enforcement.” — [Anthropic Consumer Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms)
- Privacy Policy, effective September 10, 2026, audience “Individual.” It “does not apply to content that we process on behalf of customers of our business offerings, such as our Enterprise accounts. Our use of that data is governed by our customer agreements.” URL: https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy — [Anthropic Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)
- The same privacy policy: “We may use your Inputs and Outputs to train and improve Anthropic AI models, unless you opt out through your account settings. Even if you opt-out, we will use Inputs and Outputs for model improvement when: (i) your conversations are flagged for safety review to improve our ability to detect harmful content, enforce our policies, or advance AI safety research, or (ii) you’ve explicitly reported the materials to us (for example via our feedback mechanisms).” Training sources include “Data that our users or crowd workers provide, including Inputs and Outputs from our Services (unless users opt out).” — [Anthropic Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)
- If a conversation is flagged, Anthropic “disassociate[s] the content from your user ID to train our trust and safety internal classification and generative models.” It “may re-identify the Inputs or Outputs to enforce our Terms of Service or Usage Policy.” Feedback is also “disassociate[d]” from the user ID and used “for training and improving our models.” Personal data is transferred to servers in the United States or other countries “to provide you with the Service, or to train our models.” — [Anthropic Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)
- Government ID is a separate collection path, not a ban on pasting an SSN into chat. “Verification Data”: “we may ask you to verify your age or identity,” and if the user does, Anthropic collects “an image of your government-issued identity document and the information appearing on it (such as your ID number and date of birth),” plus “your image in photo or video form” and “facial geometry templates (which may be considered ‘biometric data’ in some jurisdictions).” — [Anthropic Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)
- Consumer health-data notice is narrow: “If you are located in Washington or a state with similar consumer health data laws, please read our Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy which applies to you if you integrate third party health applications with Claude.” — [Anthropic Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)
- Usage Policy, fetched November 8, 2026, is labeled “Effective Nov 12, 2026” and applies to “anyone who submits inputs,” including “individuals using our apps (such as Claude.ai and Claude Code)” and API and reseller users. URL: https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup — [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)
- That Usage Policy prohibits uses that “Violate privacy rights as defined by applicable privacy laws, such as sharing personal information without consent or accessing private data unlawfully,” and uses that “Misuse, collect, solicit, or gain access without permission to private information such as non-public contact details, health data, biometric or neural data (including facial recognition), or confidential or proprietary data.” It also prohibits creating or disseminating “false or misleading information related to medical, health, or science issues,” and “falsified documents including fake IDs… government documents, or financial or employment records.” — [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)
- High-risk medical use is restricted, not defined as “do not type health information.” The medical category includes “diagnosing a condition; recommending, starting, stopping, or adjusting a treatment or medication,” interpreting symptoms or test results, and “assessing, diagnosing, or treating a mental health condition.” For high-risk uses, “a qualified person remain[s] accountable” and “the impacted individual knows AI was involved.” “Our products and services may not be suitable for all use cases.” — [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)
- Anthropic’s August 28, 2025 announcement of the consumer-terms change said the training choice and the longer retention apply to Claude Free, Pro, and Max, including Claude Code on those plans, and “do not apply to services under our Commercial Terms, including Claude for Work, Claude for Government, Claude for Education, or API use,” including Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud Vertex AI. It said users who allow training would have a five-year retention period for new or resumed chats, and users who do not would keep a 30-day retention period. It also said: “we use a combination of tools and automated processes to filter or obfuscate sensitive data. We do not sell users’ data to third parties.” — [Anthropic, Updates to Consumer Terms and Privacy Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)
- A later Anthropic privacy-center summary says privacy-policy changes effective July 8, 2026 “apply only to consumer accounts (Claude Free, Pro, and Max plans)” and not to Team or Enterprise, the Developer Platform, or other Commercial Terms services, and that “you can control whether your conversations are used to improve Anthropic’s AI models.” A January 12, 2026 summary says a Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy link was added for users in states with consumer health data laws who integrate third-party health apps. — [Anthropic Privacy Center, updates](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/10301952-updates-to-our-privacy-policy)

### Inferences

- If health data or an SSN is submitted as a Claude.ai Input, the September 10, 2026 privacy policy and the October 8, 2025 consumer terms treat it as material that may be used for training unless the user has opted out. Opt-out does not cover the safety-flag and explicit-feedback exceptions written into both documents. Those exceptions are the interaction that matters: a sensitive prompt that trips a safety classifier can still be used for model improvement after the user opted out.
- The consumer health-data policy, as described, is triggered by integrating a third-party health app in certain states. It is not, on the pages fetched, a general rule that ordinary chat is covered by a health-privacy statute or by a BAA.
- The Usage Policy’s health-data sentence is about misuse and access “without permission.” It does not, in the text fetched, say that a person may not paste their own records. It also does not say that a filtered answer from a TEE is exempt from the training rules if that answer, or the raw record, is in fact submitted to Claude.
- Commercial and Enterprise processing is a different contract. Nothing fetched here says consumer Claude.ai, Claude Pro, or Claude Max comes with a HIPAA BAA.

### Gaps

- The Usage Policy effective date on the page is November 12, 2026. The prior version was not fetched. The prohibitions quoted above are the text displayed on November 8, 2026, which the page itself marks as not yet effective.
- The five-year and 30-day retention figures are from Anthropic’s 2025 announcement. They were not found in the portions reviewed of the September 10, 2026 privacy policy. Treat the numbers as announced in 2025, not as re-confirmed in the current policy text.
- No Anthropic page was fetched that states whether Claude for Work, Enterprise, or the API currently offers a HIPAA BAA. The consumer documents fetched do not offer one. Do not fill that gap by assuming a commercial BAA exists or does not exist.
- The Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy itself was not fetched, only the pointer to it.

## A3. Google consumer Gemini and consumer Drive

### Takeaway

Google’s own Gemini Apps notice, last updated October 26, 2026, tells users not to enter confidential information they would not want a reviewer to see or Google to use, and it says chats can be used to train models and can be read by human reviewers. Google’s HIPAA BAA is a Google Cloud and Google Workspace agreement for listed covered services. It is not a feature of the consumer Gemini app or of a personal Google account. Workspace client-side encryption is a separate admin feature under which Google says it cannot decrypt those files. That is not the same product as a household app encrypting files before uploading them to consumer Drive.

### Cited Findings

- Gemini Apps Privacy Hub, “Last updated: October 26, 2026” (one section on the same page is marked last updated October 13, 2026). Google says human reviewers, “including trained reviewers from our service providers,” review some data, and: “Please don’t enter confidential information that you wouldn’t want a reviewer to see or Google to use to improve our services, including machine-learning technologies.” URL: https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en — [Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en)
- If Keep Activity is on, “Google uses your activity to provide, develop, and improve its services (including training generative AI models),” including “with the help of human reviewers.” “Data reviewed by service providers is disconnected from your account and saved for 3 years.” Default auto-delete of activity is 18 months, changeable to 3 months, 36 months, or not auto-deleted. — [Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en)
- If Keep Activity is off, future chats “won’t be used to train our AI models, unless you choose to send Google feedback,” but they “are still saved for 72 hours” so Gemini can respond, process feedback, and protect Google and users. “Even if your Keep Activity setting is off or you use temporary chats, Google still uses your chats to respond to you and help protect Google, our users, and the public, including with help from human reviewers.” Temporary chats “are not used to train Google’s AI models.” Chats already reviewed are not deleted with the user’s activity; they can be kept up to three years. — [Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en)
- The Gemini Apps Privacy Hub text that was fetched does not offer a HIPAA BAA and does not say consumer Gemini is a covered service. — [Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en)
- Google’s HIPAA materials that were fetched are Cloud and Workspace documents. The BAA “is entered into between Google LLC (‘Google’) and the customer agreeing to the terms” and “will be effective when Customer clicks to accept this BAA.” “This BAA does not apply to any Google product, service, or feature that is not a Covered Service.” It applies to the extent the customer is a covered entity or business associate and Google, as a result, is a business associate or subcontractor. URL: https://cloud.google.com/terms/hipaa-baa — [Google Cloud HIPAA BAA](https://cloud.google.com/terms/hipaa-baa)
- Google’s HIPAA implementation page lists Google Workspace services “included in the Google Cloud BAA,” including “Gemini App (excluding Gemini in Google Chrome),” “Gemini in Google Workspace,” “Gemini Mac App,” and “Google Drive (including Google Docs, Google Forms, Google Photos, Google Sheets, Google Slides, and Google Vids).” The page says Google Cloud and Google Workspace “support HIPAA compliance within the scope of a Business Associate Agreement, but ultimately you are responsible for evaluating your own HIPAA compliance.” URL: https://docs.cloud.google.com/docs/security/compliance/hipaa — [Google Cloud, HIPAA compliance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/docs/security/compliance/hipaa)
- HHS says a cloud provider that stores encrypted ePHI for a covered entity or business associate is still generally a business associate, “even if the CSP cannot view the ePHI because it is encrypted and the CSP does not have the decryption key.” The same FAQ says: “OCR does not endorse, certify, or recommend specific technology or products.” Content last reviewed February 15, 2023. — [HHS OCR, FAQ on cloud services](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/2078/which-csps-offer-hipaa-compliant-cloud-services/index.html)
- Drive help page on encrypted files: “All files uploaded to Drive or created in Docs, Sheets, and Slides are encrypted in transit and at rest with AES256 bit encryption.” Additional confidentiality is “Workspace Client-side encryption,” which an administrator enables. “Normally Google encrypts your content in transit and at rest, but with client-side encryption your domain has chosen to add an extra layer of protection.” For that Workspace feature: “Google cannot decrypt your files.” The same page says local applications with sufficient permissions “may be able to view and exfiltrate encrypted files,” and “Encryption does not protect your files from someone who can view your screen.” URL: https://support.google.com/drive/answer/10519333?hl=en — [Google Drive Help, encrypted files](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/10519333?hl=en)
- A vendor blog states that consumer Gemini at gemini.google.com is outside the BAA. That is a secondary, commercially interested source. It is recorded only because it matches the product split on Google’s own pages. It is not authority. — [Strac, “Is Gemini HIPAA Compliant?”](https://www.strac.io/blog/is-gemini-hipaa-compliant)

### Inferences

- Putting health data or SSNs into the consumer Gemini app is the situation the October 26, 2026 notice warns against: reviewers and model-improvement use. Turning Keep Activity off reduces training use. It does not, on Google’s own words, stop 72-hour storage or safety review by humans.
- A personal Google account does not become a Workspace BAA because the user uploads a file to Drive or asks Gemini a question. The BAA text is a click-accept customer agreement for Covered Services. Consumer Gemini and consumer Drive were not listed, on the pages fetched, as products that come with that agreement already in place.
- If the household device encrypts files before upload and Google never has the key, that is the device’s encryption, not Google’s Workspace CSE product. Google’s CSE sentence “Google cannot decrypt your files” is a statement about Workspace CSE. It is fair to describe a true client-side design as “encrypted on the device before upload.” It is not fair to import Google’s CSE sentence, or to say Google “cannot see” metadata, filenames, account logs, or ciphertext, unless the design and Google’s current Drive behavior actually support each of those points. This note did not verify consumer Drive metadata practices.
- If a covered entity later used this device, or used Google, to hold patient records, HHS’s cloud FAQ is the relevant warning: encryption without giving Google the key does not by itself take a cloud provider outside “business associate” status when the provider stores ePHI for a covered entity. That FAQ does not turn a household’s own files into ePHI.

### Gaps

- https://policies.google.com/privacy and https://policies.google.com/terms were not fetched. There is no verbatim quote here of the general Google Privacy Policy sentence on content users upload to Drive.
- The Cloud HIPAA page was fetched, but no “last updated” line from that page was recorded. The BAA page was fetched without a separate revision date beyond the contract text.
- No page was fetched that says, in a single sentence, “consumer Drive does not offer a HIPAA BAA.” The finding is the scope of the BAA that was fetched.
- Consumer Drive’s current handling of filenames, previews, and malware scanning for already-encrypted blobs was not fetched.

## A4. IRS Publication 4557, Publication 1075, IRC 7216, and the FTC Safeguards Rule

### Takeaway

A family storing its own tax returns is not a tax preparer and is not the audience of these rules. A software maker is not a preparer merely because a household can keep copies of its own returns in the software. Publication 4557 is IRS education for tax professionals. Publication 1075 binds agencies that receive federal tax information from the IRS. The binding hooks for preparers are the FTC Safeguards Rule, 16 CFR Part 314, and IRC 7216 plus Treas. Reg. 301.7216-1. Those hooks would matter if the product were later held out as tax-preparation or e-file software, or marketed so that a preparer uses it to hold client return information.

### Cited Findings

- Publication 4557, as fetched, addresses “tax professionals” and “Authorized IRS e-File Provider[s].” It says “Protecting taxpayer data is the law” and points to FTC authority and the Safeguards Rule. It says: “The ‘financial institutions’ definition includes professional tax preparers.” It tells that audience to encrypt sensitive files and, if using cloud storage, to encrypt before upload. The closing note says the IRS “prepared this guide as an outreach educational effort for all tax preparers, transmitters, and software developers.” The PDF footer reads “Publication 4557 (Rev. 6-2024).” A search-result title for the same URL said “Rev. 5-2024.” Use the PDF footer, and re-check the cover if the file is updated. URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4557.pdf — [IRS Publication 4557](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4557.pdf)
- IRS web guidance to tax professionals: “Federal Trade Commission regulations require professional tax preparers to create and enact security plans to protect client data. Review IRS Publication 4557.” — [IRS, Protect your clients; protect yourself](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/protect-your-clients-protect-yourself)
- Publication 1075 title: “Tax Information Security Guidelines For Federal, State and Local Agencies” and “Safeguards for Protecting Federal Tax Returns and Return Information.” The Office of Safeguards mission in the fetched text is ensuring confidentiality of “IRS information provided to federal, state, and local agencies” and compliance with IRC 6103(p)(4) by “external government agencies” that hold federal tax information. The revision fetched is “Publication 1075 (Rev. 11-2021),” which “revises and supersedes Publication 1075 (November 2016).” URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1075.pdf — [IRS Publication 1075](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1075.pdf)
- 16 CFR 314.1(b), current eCFR text fetched November 8, 2026: the Safeguards Rule “applies to the handling of customer information by all financial institutions over which the Federal Trade Commission has jurisdiction.” The enumerated examples “include, but are not limited to,” mortgage lenders, payday lenders, finance companies, and “tax preparation firms,” among others. A “consumer” in the rule is an individual who obtains a financial product or service “primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” The rule requires a written information security program. URL: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-314 — [16 CFR Part 314](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-314)
- IRC 7216(a): the misdemeanor applies to “Any person who is engaged in the business of preparing, or providing services in connection with the preparation of, returns of the tax imposed by chapter 1, or any person who for compensation prepares any such return for any other person,” who knowingly or recklessly discloses information furnished for preparation of such a return, or uses it for a purpose other than preparing or assisting in preparing the return. URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7216 — [26 USC 7216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7216)
- Treas. Reg. 301.7216-1(b)(2), as fetched from the eCFR text on LII, defines “tax return preparer” for section 7216 more broadly than the household user. It includes a person “engaged in the business of preparing or assisting in preparing tax returns,” a person “engaged in the business of providing auxiliary services in connection with the preparation of tax returns, including a person who develops software that is used to prepare or file a tax return and any Authorized IRS e-file Provider,” a person “otherwise compensated for preparing, or assisting in preparing, a tax return for any other person,” and certain employees of those persons. — [26 CFR 301.7216-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-1)
- The same regulation: a person provides auxiliary services if, in the course of business, the person “holds himself out to tax return preparers or to taxpayers as a person who performs auxiliary services,” or receives tax return information from another preparer under the cross-referenced consent rule. Exclusions: a person is not a preparer “merely because” they lease space, furnish credit, furnish information to a preparer at the taxpayer’s request, link to someone else’s tax site, “or otherwise perform some service that only incidentally relates to the preparation of tax returns.” Example 1 in the tax-return-information definition is software “designed to assist with the preparation and filing” of a return: the provider is a preparer under (b)(2)(i)(B), and registration information the taxpayer provides is tax return information. The regulation’s applicability date for this section is disclosures or uses on or after January 1, 2009. — [26 CFR 301.7216-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-1)
- The regulation also says GLBA requirements and section 7216 sit side by side. Neither displaces the other. — [26 CFR 301.7216-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-1)

### Inferences

- A family keeping its own returns, SSNs, and forms on its own device is not “engaged in the business” of preparing returns for another person and is not being paid to prepare someone else’s return. Publication 1075 does not apply, because the family is not a government agency receiving federal tax information under IRC 6103. The Safeguards Rule’s listed “tax preparation firms” are firms, not households. Publication 4557 is guidance to tax professionals. It is not a statute that converts a household into a preparer.
- A software maker is not a section 7216 preparer only because the software can store a return the family already has. The regulation’s software example is software “used to prepare or file a tax return,” by someone who holds themselves out as providing those auxiliary services. Storage of family copies, without holding the product out as preparation or e-file software, is outside that example on the text fetched. The incidental-services exclusion points the same direction, but it is an exclusion from the preparer definition, not a safe harbor drafted for this product.
- Publication 4557’s closing nod to “software developers” is the pamphlet’s audience. It is not a finding that every developer of software that can hold a PDF of a Form 1040 is a financial institution under 16 CFR 314.
- If the product were later marketed to tax preparers or bookkeepers as a place to hold client returns, or held out as software used to prepare or file returns, the line moves. The preparer customer would be a financial institution under the Safeguards Rule and would have to choose service providers and contract for safeguards. The maker could itself fall under Treas. Reg. 301.7216-1(b)(2)(i)(B) if it is in the business of providing that auxiliary service, including developing software used to prepare or file returns. Disclosure of client return information to a model, a cloud operator, or a backup provider would then be a 7216 disclosure question, with criminal and related civil penalties under sections 7216 and 6713, not merely a privacy-policy question. Publication 4557 would remain guidance. The Safeguards Rule and IRC 7216 would be the binding hooks.

### Gaps

- 26 USC 7701(a)(36), the narrower “tax return preparer” definition used for preparer penalties other than 7216, was not fetched. The 7216 regulation itself says someone can be a 7216 preparer without being a 7701(a)(36) preparer. This note does not quote 7701.
- No IRS document was fetched that uses the words “a family storing its own return is not a preparer.” That sentence is the inference from the statutory and regulatory definitions above.
- Whether a future paid bookkeeper product would also be a “financial institution” under GLBA for reasons other than tax preparation was not researched.

## B1. Section 5 deception standard

### Takeaway

A privacy or safety claim is a Section 5 problem when it is likely to mislead a reasonable consumer and it matters to the decision to use or buy the product. Implied claims count. The 1983 policy statement is the Commission’s articulation of that test. The PDF itself 404’d on this fetch, so the elements below are the standard formulation of that statement, not a sentence copied from a successful download of the PDF.

### Cited Findings

- The Commission’s library records an “FTC Policy Statement on Deception” dated October 14, 1983. The historical PDF URL returned 404. — [FTC library listing, as mirrored](https://digest.law/commercial-and-trade-law/consumer-protection-law/federal-regulatory-enforcement-agencies/ftc-sec-and-cfpb-jurisdiction/sources/ftc-policy-statement-deception/)
- The GoodRx complaint pleads an implied claim. It alleges the HIPAA seal “signaled to users that it is a HIPAA-covered entity, and that its practices complied with HIPAA’s requirements,” and it charges a representation “directly or indirectly, expressly or by implication.” — [GoodRx complaint (PDF)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.407839/gov.uscourts.cand.407839.1.0.pdf)
- Khan’s fetched statement says exaggerated claims about AI capabilities break the law if they are not supported by concrete evidence, and that claims must hold true across consumer demographics. — [Khan statement (PDF)](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/remarks-of-chair-lina-m-khan-re-joint-interagency-statement-on-ai.pdf)
- The .com Disclosures guide and the advertising-substantiation policy statement were not fetched. See Gaps in A5.

### Inferences

- The working test, which the report should attribute to the 1983 Policy Statement on Deception and flag as not re-downloaded, is: a claim is deceptive if it is a representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead consumers acting reasonably in the circumstances, and the representation is material. Material means it is likely to affect the decision to buy or use the product. Implied claims and omissions count. A reasonable consumer need not be an expert in TEEs, HIPAA, or provider training settings.
- Substantiation, as the Khan statement and the secondary Atleson summaries describe it, is prior support for the claim a reasonable consumer takes away. A true mechanism plus an absolute headline can still mislead if the headline says more than the mechanism does.
- Health data, SSNs, passports, and a teenager’s records are the kind of facts a reasonable consumer treats as important. A sentence about who can see them is material if it is used to promote the device.

## B2. Why “HIPAA compliant” is a high-risk claim for this device

### Takeaway

HHS does not certify products as “HIPAA compliant.” HIPAA applies to covered entities and to business associates who handle protected health information for them. A consumer household device that stores the family’s own records, and a hackathon project that is not providing that service for a doctor, health plan, or their contractor, is outside that status on the HHS materials fetched. Saying “HIPAA compliant” implies a legal status and a government oversight regime the product does not have. The GoodRx complaint shows the FTC treating that implication as deception.

### Cited Findings

- HHS OCR, page last reviewed July 26, 2013, and still the page returned for this topic: “HHS and OCR do not endorse any private consultants' or education providers' seminars, materials or systems, and do not certify any persons or products as ‘HIPAA compliant.’” URL: https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/be-aware-misleading-marketing-claims/index.html — [HHS OCR, misleading marketing claims](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/be-aware-misleading-marketing-claims/index.html)

### Inferences

- For this hackathon device, the accurate orientation is: storing a family’s own health records, including a teenager’s, does not make the family or the open-source project a HIPAA covered entity. HHS says it does not issue that certification.

It does say that copying a cloud vendor’s phrase “designed to support HIPAA compliance” onto a consumer device would import a claim about a status the consumer product does not have.

## B3. Why absolute exposure claims are high-risk, and safer wording

### Takeaway

“Zero data exposure” and “nobody can see your data” are absolute. The fetched provider terms and the residual paths in ordinary systems make those sentences false if a reasonable consumer hears them as “disclosure is impossible.” Safer wording names the mechanism and the residual risk.

An absolute claim is still misleading if a reasonable consumer would think it covers the TEE operator, the host, logs, a bug that sends the raw record, Google account metadata on a backup, or a later change in provider terms.
- “Client-side encrypted before it is uploaded” describes a mechanism. It does not say Google cannot see filenames, sizes, or account activity, and it does not say the key cannot be lost or stolen on the device.
- “We do not receive your records in the ordinary design” is a statement about the operator of this device. It is not a promise that disclosure is impossible.
- “This is not the same as a promise that disclosure is impossible” is the kind of limit that keeps a mechanism sentence from becoming an absolute claim.

### Gaps

- No consumer survey or FTC closing letter was fetched on the specific phrases “zero data exposure” or “nobody can see your data.” The risk assessment is the deception standard applied to those words, not a case with that caption.

## B4. Hackathon README versus paid advertising

### Takeaway

Section 5 is about deceptive acts in commerce, not only about paid ads. A README that promotes the project with absolute safety or HIPAA claims can be read as advertising. A README that states what the software does, what it does not promise, and that it is not legal, medical, or tax advice is a different document.

### Cited Findings

- The GoodRx complaint frames the HIPAA seal as a representation “in connection with the advertising, distribution, or sale” of the service, including a seal on a homepage. The representation did not have to be a television ad. — [GoodRx complaint (PDF)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.407839/gov.uscourts.cand.407839.1.0.pdf)
- Khan’s statement, quoted in A5, treats unsupported AI capability claims as a deception problem. It is not limited to paid media. — [Khan statement (PDF)](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/remarks-of-chair-lina-m-khan-re-joint-interagency-statement-on-ai.pdf)
- OpenAI’s Usage Policies say their rules “are no substitute for legal requirements, professional duties, or ethical obligations.” Anthropic’s Usage Policy says its high-risk requirements “do not replace local legal requirements” and that the products “may not be suitable for all use cases.” Those are the providers’ own limits on what the model output is. — [OpenAI Usage Policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/); [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)

### Inferences

- An open-source hackathon README is not immune because it is unpaid or because the authors are students. If it is used to invite families to put SSNs and health records into the device, the sentences that describe safety are promotional claims. Section 5 still applies when the project is offered in commerce. A purely technical description of a local prototype, not held out as safe for other people’s tax or health files, is a weaker case. The difference is what a reasonable reader is invited to believe.
- A useful README states the data path, the residual risk, and a non-advice line: the software is not legal, medical, or tax advice, and it is not a HIPAA program. That is a description. It is not a promise that a regulator has approved the device.

### Gaps

- No case was fetched that holds, in those words, that a GitHub README is an advertisement. The inference uses the Commission’s “advertising, distribution, or sale” framing in GoodRx and the ordinary deception test.

## Claims that are dangerous to make, and safer wording

Each dangerous line is a claim a reasonable consumer can take as a promise. The safer line is tied to the reason it is narrower.

- Dangerous: “HIPAA compliant.” Reason: HHS does not certify products as HIPAA compliant, and this consumer household device is not, on the facts given, a covered entity or business associate. The GoodRx complaint treats a HIPAA seal as an implied claim of covered-entity status and of compliance. Safer: “This project is not a HIPAA product and is not offered as a HIPAA business associate.” If a mechanism must be mentioned: “It is designed so a consumer assistant receives a checked answer rather than the raw record. That design is not a HIPAA certification.”
- Dangerous: “HIPAA certified” or “HHS certified.” Reason: OCR’s posted warning says HHS and OCR “do not certify any persons or products as ‘HIPAA compliant,’” and OCR “does not endorse, certify, or recommend specific technology or products.” Safer: do not use “certified.” If a third-party audit exists someday, name the auditor and the scope, and say it is not an HHS certification.
- Dangerous: “Zero data exposure.” Reason: the phrase says exposure is zero. Residual paths that the fetched materials leave open include the TEE operator and host, logs, bugs, legal process, Google metadata on a backup, and, if raw text actually reaches them, consumer ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini training and human review. Safer: “In the ordinary design, the assistant receives only a checked answer. The model that handles the records is designed to run in a TEE operated by NEAR AI. This is not a promise that disclosure is impossible.”
- Dangerous: “Nobody can see your data.” Reason: “nobody” includes the operator, a cloud admin, Google, a reviewer, and an attacker who exploits a bug. A TEE plus a filter does not establish that. Safer: “We do not receive your records in the ordinary design. The assistant is not supposed to receive the raw file. People who operate the TEE provider, and anyone who compromises the device or the backup, are outside that sentence.”
- Dangerous: “ChatGPT and Claude never see sensitive data, so their training policies do not matter.” Reason: their policies apply to what those services actually receive. If the filter fails, or if the checked answer itself contains an SSN or a diagnosis, the consumer terms on training, safety review, and retention apply. Those consumer plans are not the API BAA and not zero-data retention. Safer: “The design intent is that ChatGPT and Claude receive a filtered answer, not the file. If sensitive text is included in what they receive, their consumer terms allow training unless the user has opted out, and Anthropic’s opt-out still has a safety-review exception. This project does not turn a consumer account into an enterprise BAA.”
- Dangerous: “End-to-end encrypted” or “Google cannot see the backup,” if what is true is only that the device encrypts before upload. Reason: Google’s “Google cannot decrypt your files” sentence is about Workspace client-side encryption, a different product. Ordinary Drive encryption is in transit and at rest with Google’s normal access to the plaintext. Safer: “Files are encrypted on the device before they are uploaded. Google still has the account and whatever metadata Drive stores. This is not Google’s Workspace client-side encryption product, and it is not a promise that metadata exposure is impossible.”
- Dangerous: “IRS safeguarded” or “Publication 4557 compliant,” for a family device. Reason: Publication 4557 is guidance for tax professionals. Publication 1075 is for agencies that receive federal tax information. A family storing its own returns is not a preparer. Using the IRS name implies a status and an audit program the project does not have. Safer: “A household copy of your own return is not a tax-preparer engagement.
