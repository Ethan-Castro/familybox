# FTC Health Breach Notification Rule

Orientation, not legal advice. Current as of November 8, 2026. The regulatory text below is the eCFR text of 16 CFR Part 318 fetched that day. The eCFR is a continuously updated unofficial compilation; the official text is the Federal Register.

## Current text, dates, and developments through November 2026

### Takeaway
The Health Breach Notification Rule, 16 CFR Part 318, was issued in 2009 and substantively amended once, by a final rule published May 30, 2024, effective July 29, 2024. No later amendment of the regulatory text was found. On September 9, 2026 the FTC withdrew its 2021 policy statement as obsolete because the 2024 rule had already addressed health apps; that withdrawal did not repeal the 2024 text. No court decision vacating the 2024 rule was found.

### Cited Findings
- The rule implements section 13407 of the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, 42 U.S.C. 17937. Authority cited in the eCFR is 42 U.S.C. 17937 and 17953. Source note: “74 FR 42980, Aug. 25, 2009, as amended at 89 FR 47054, May 30, 2024, unless otherwise noted.” — [eCFR 16 CFR Part 318](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318) (fetched November 8, 2026).
- 2009 final rule: Federal Register document E9-20142, RIN 3084-AB17, 74 FR 42962 (Aug. 25, 2009), pages 42962–42985. “This rule is effective September 24, 2009. Full compliance is required by February 22, 2010.” The eCFR’s “74 FR 42980” pinpoints the codified text inside that document, not a different rule. — [2009 Federal Register final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule).
- 2024 final amendments: Federal Register document 2024-10855, RIN 3084-AB56, 89 FR 47028 (May 30, 2024), pages 47028–47064. DATES: “The amendments are effective July 29, 2024.” The DATES line states an effective date and does not add a later “full compliance” date of the 2009 type. The preamble lists eight changes: scope including many health apps; what “draw … from multiple sources” means; breach includes a data breach or an unauthorized disclosure; PHR related entity; method of notice; content of notice; timing of notice to the FTC; readability, including a penalties section. — [2024 Federal Register final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Scope, 16 CFR 318.1(a): the rule “applies to foreign and domestic vendors of personal health records, PHR related entities, and third party service providers, irrespective of any jurisdictional tests in the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Act, that maintain information of U.S. citizens or residents. This part does not apply to HIPAA-covered entities, or to any other entity to the extent that it engages in activities as a business associate of a HIPAA-covered entity.” Section 318.1(b) preempts state law “as set forth in” 42 U.S.C. 17951. Section 318.8: the part applies to breaches discovered on or after September 24, 2009. Section 318.9 sunsets the part for breaches discovered on or after the effective date of regulations implementing later legislation that imposes breach-notification requirements on the same entities. — [eCFR Part 318](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- September 15, 2021: the Commission issued a policy statement that, as the 2024 preamble describes it, said the rule covers most non-HIPAA health apps and similar technologies that are capable of drawing information from multiple sources, and that a breach is “not limited to cybersecurity intrusions or nefarious behavior.” — [89 FR 47028 preamble](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- September 9, 2026: Hunton Andrews Kurth (September 16, 2026) reports the FTC rescinded the “2021 Policy Statement on Breaches by Health Apps and Other Connected Devices,” and that the FTC “concluded that the Statement is obsolete because its 2024 amendments to the Rule expressly addressed health apps, connected devices, and unauthorized disclosures,” consistent with avoiding unnecessary subregulatory guidance. The American Hospital Association (September 10, 2026) reports the same announcement and the same reason: the 2021 statement “was superseded by its 2024 update.” — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-rescinds-2021-policy-statement-on-health-app-data-breaches); [AHA](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-09-10-ftc-rescinds-policy-statement-extending-breach-notification-rule-apps-devices-collect-health).
- Epstein Becker Green reads that rescission as ending FTC endorsement of three 2021 positions: treating app developers as health care providers, the multiple-source aggregation theory, and treating unauthorized sharing as a breach. It states those features “no longer apply.” — [Epstein Becker Green](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/ftc-reverses-course-on-health-app-privacy). That reading conflicts with Hunton and the AHA, which describe the withdrawal as housekeeping because the 2024 rule already codified those points, and it conflicts with the eCFR text still containing the 2024 definitions (source note ends at 89 FR 47054).
- A `site:ftc.gov` search for the September 2026 rescission returned no results in this session. The FTC press-release URL was not retrieved. No source reviewed reported a Federal Register amendment of Part 318 after May 30, 2024, or a court decision vacating the 2024 rule.

### Inferences
- The binding instrument as of November 8, 2026 is the 2024 codified rule, not the withdrawn 2021 policy statement. Withdrawing guidance that the agency says was superseded by a rule does not delete the rule.
- Epstein Becker’s claim that unauthorized-disclosure breaches and health-app coverage “no longer apply” should not be used as a statement of current law unless a later rule, statute, or court decision is produced. None was found here.

### Gaps
- The FTC’s own September 9, 2026 press release or Commission vote was not opened. Secondary reports agree on the date and on what was withdrawn; they disagree on legal effect.
- No PACER or comprehensive enforcement-docket search was done. Absence of a reported challenge is not proof that no case was filed.
- Whether any bill triggering the section 318.9 sunset has been enacted was not separately researched. The eCFR still publishes Part 318 in full.

## Definitions that decide coverage

### Takeaway
Coverage requires a personal health record: an electronic record of PHR identifiable health information, with the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources, managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual. A vendor is an entity that offers or maintains that record. A breach is unauthorized acquisition of unsecured PHR identifiable health information, and since July 29, 2024 the text expressly includes an unauthorized disclosure as well as a hack. At least one source must supply health information; a second source can be non-health information drawn through a technical integration, and unused capacity still counts.

### Cited Findings
- Personal health record, 16 CFR 318.2: “an electronic record of PHR identifiable health information on an individual that has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources and that is managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual.” The 2009 text said “that can be drawn from multiple sources.” The 2024 amendment changed that phrase to “has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources.” — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- PHR identifiable health information, 16 CFR 318.2, means information that: “(1) Relates to the past, present, or future physical or mental health or condition of an individual, the provision of health care to an individual, or the past, present, or future payment for the provision of health care to an individual; and (i) Identifies the individual; or (ii) With respect to which there is a reasonable basis to believe that the information can be used to identify the individual; and (2) Is created or received by a: (i) Covered health care provider; (ii) Health plan (as defined in 42 U.S.C. 1320d(5)); (iii) Employer; or (iv) Health care clearinghouse (as defined in 42 U.S.C. 1320d(2)); and (3) With respect to an individual, includes information that is provided by or on behalf of the individual.” The 2024 preamble says this reorder was organizational, to track the Recovery Act and to “preserve the word ‘includes’” for information provided by or on behalf of the individual. — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Covered health care provider, 16 CFR 318.2: a provider of services under 42 U.S.C. 1395x(u), a provider of medical or other health services under 42 U.S.C. 1395x(s), “or any other entity furnishing health care services or supplies.” Health care services or supplies “means any online service such as a website, mobile application, or internet-connected device that provides mechanisms to track diseases, health conditions, diagnoses or diagnostic testing, treatment, medications, vital signs, symptoms, bodily functions, fitness, fertility, sexual health, sleep, mental health, genetic information, diet, or that provides other health-related services or tools.” The Commission said “covered health care provider” is unique to this rule and does not control the term in other agencies’ regulations. It kept the catch-all so the definition can cover later technology, read in the context of the tracking list that precedes it. — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47035](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Vendor of personal health records, 16 CFR 318.2: “an entity, other than a HIPAA-covered entity or an entity to the extent that it engages in activities as a business associate of a HIPAA-covered entity, that offers or maintains a personal health record.” The 2024 preamble glosses “vendor of” as entities “in the business of offering or maintaining (e.g., selling, marketing, providing, or promoting)” a health-related product or service. “To be a vendor of personal health records under the Rule, an app, website, or online service must provide an offering that relates more than tangentially to health.” A general retailer’s purchase-history app is only tangential. There is no electronic record, and thus no PHR, for a brick-and-mortar purchase with no app, website, or online service. — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47035](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- PHR related entity, 16 CFR 318.2: an entity, other than a HIPAA-covered entity or a business associate to the extent of those activities, that “(1) Offers products or services through the website, including any online service, of a vendor of personal health records; (2) Offers products or services through the websites, including any online service, of HIPAA-covered entities that offer individuals personal health records; or (3) Accesses unsecured PHR identifiable health information in a personal health record or sends unsecured PHR identifiable health information to a personal health record.” The 2024 change added “any online service” (so mobile apps count, not only websites) and limited prong (3) to unsecured PHR identifiable health information rather than “any information.” Examples of related entities include a remote blood-pressure cuff, connected glucose monitor, or fitness tracker synced to a health app. A grocery delivery service that sends food-purchase information and does not access unsecured PHR identifiable health information is not one. — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47028 preamble, PHR related entity discussion](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Third party service provider, 16 CFR 318.2: an entity that “(1) Provides services to a vendor of personal health records in connection with the offering or maintenance of a personal health record or to a PHR related entity in connection with a product or service offered by that entity; and (2) Accesses, maintains, retains, modifies, records, stores, destroys, or otherwise holds, uses, or discloses unsecured PHR identifiable health information as a result of such services.” The 2009 preamble’s examples are billing, debt collection, and data storage, and it uses a cloud computing provider that may not know customers host PHRs. The 2024 preamble’s examples of service providers are support and administrative functions, including attribution and analytics. Section 318.3(b) states: “While some third party service providers may access unsecured PHR identifiable health information in the course of providing services, this does not render the third party service provider a PHR related entity.” The Commission did not want one firm to be both, because notice duties would conflict. — [eCFR 318.2 and 318.3(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [74 FR 42962](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule); [89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Breach of security, 16 CFR 318.2: “acquisition of such information without the authorization of the individual.” Unauthorized acquisition is presumed from unauthorized access unless the entity “has reliable evidence showing that there has not been, or could not reasonably have been, unauthorized acquisition.” The 2024 sentence: “A breach of security includes an unauthorized acquisition of unsecured PHR identifiable health information in a personal health record that occurs as a result of a data breach or an unauthorized disclosure.” The preamble says this covers “a company’s unauthorized sharing or selling of consumers’ information to third parties that is inconsistent with the company’s representations to consumers,” and “a voluntary disclosure made by the PHR vendor or PHR related entity where such disclosure was not authorized by the consumer.” The Commission did not define “authorization.” It pointed to the 2009 commentary and the GoodRx and Easy Healthcare orders: buried disclosures and dark patterns are not meaningful choice; a disclosure inconsistent with privacy promises is unauthorized. The 2009 commentary: use that enhances the PHR experience is within authorization if consistent with disclosures and reasonable expectations, including “data processing … through the use of service providers.” Beyond that, sharing requires meaningful choice. “Buried disclosures in lengthy privacy policies do not satisfy the standard of ‘meaningful choice.’” — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47038–47041](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule); [74 FR 42967–42968](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Unsecured, 16 CFR 318.2: PHR identifiable information “not protected through the use of a technology or methodology specified by the Secretary of Health and Human Services in the guidance issued under section 13402(h)(2) of the American Reinvestment and Recovery Act of 2009, 42 U.S.C. 17932(h)(2).” The 2009 preamble removed a fallback ANSI definition because HHS had already issued that guidance. The 2024 preamble: the rule “is triggered only by breaches of unsecured PHR identifiable health information” and “creates appropriate incentives” to secure information “through specified encryption methods.” De-identified data under HHS standards is not PHR identifiable health information. The 2009 commentary rejected the idea that name and credit-card data alone are PHR identifiable health information, unless the disclosure identifies the person as a customer of a vendor associated with a particular health condition. — [eCFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [74 FR 42968–42970](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule); [89 FR 47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Multiple sources, as adopted. A product is a PHR if it can draw from multiple sources even when a given consumer uses only one. “Either the app has the technical means (e.g., the application programming interface or API) to draw information from multiple sources, or it does not.” It can qualify “if it can draw any information from multiple sources, even if it only draws health information from one source.” The Commission “declines to exempt apps and services where there are available but unused or unpublicized APIs or integrations” and declines to exempt beta testing. “A product feature or integration that exists and that is able to draw PHR identifiable health information counts as a source.” It “does not intend to cover instances where an app can draw from multiple sources only through changes to the design or underlying software code and where the app developer does not implement those changes.” “The Rule still requires drawing PHR identifiable health information from at least one source.” The record must still be “managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual.” — [89 FR 47036–47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Preamble examples at 89 FR 47035–47038:
  - Health-advice site A, purely informational, no mechanism to track or record: not a PHR, because there is no electronic record of PHR identifiable health information on an individual.
  - Symptom tracker B, username and password, consumer-entered symptoms, and the site itself is the health care provider that receives them: an electronic record of PHR identifiable health information, but “not a personal health record to the extent the site does not have the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources (i.e., if the consumer is its only source of information).”
  - Same tracker plus a geolocation API: a PHR. Sources are “consumer inputs and collection of geolocation data through the API.”
  - Same tracker plus a data broker: a PHR. Sources are “the consumer and the data broker.”
  - Diet app that can sync a third-party wearable: a PHR “even if some users elect not to connect the fitness tracker.”
  - Diet app that can pull calendar entries through a calendar API to personalize meal suggestions: a PHR, because it draws identifiable health information from the user and non-health information from the calendar.
  - “Whether a health app or other electronic record constitutes a personal health record … is a fact-intensive inquiry.” — [89 FR 47035–47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Covington (May 2024) summarizes the same points: technical capacity is enough even if some users never connect the second source, and the vendor threshold is an offering that “relates more than tangentially to health.” — [Covington Digital Health](https://www.covingtondigitalhealth.com/2024/05/ftc-issues-final-rule-to-expand-scope-of-the-health-breach-notification-rule/). A JD Supra analysis of the final rule states that only one of the sources must supply health information, and that unused or beta APIs still count. — [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ftc-vastly-expands-reach-of-the-health-9169285/).

### Inferences
- “Draws information from multiple sources” is a technical-capacity test about feeds into the record. It is not a test of how many institutions originally created the documents, and it is not satisfied by the mere possibility of writing new code.
- The second source need not be a hospital, pharmacy, or health app. A non-health API is enough if one source supplies PHR identifiable health information.
- “Includes information that is provided by or on behalf of the individual” adds user-supplied information. It does not, on the preamble’s account, drop information created by a provider.

### Gaps
- The current text of the HHS encryption-and-destruction guidance incorporated by the definition of “unsecured” was not re-fetched. Whether a confidential-computing TEE, as opposed to encryption that leaves the holder without the key, meets that guidance was not found in FTC or HHS sources reviewed.
- The Commission gave no example of a local, open-source, self-hosted family appliance, a file the user uploads from a cloud drive, a PDF, or an AI model as a “source.”

## Does this device “draw information from multiple sources”?

### Takeaway
A build whose only source is the family loading records, and that has no implemented integration to another system, matches the FTC’s own example of something that is not a personal health record. An implemented import or sync from Apple Health, a hospital portal, a lab system, or a cloud API is a second source even if a particular family never turns it on. A user-uploaded hospital export or lab PDF is still the consumer as the source. An AI model that only answers questions about records already stored is a processor, not one of the sources in the FTC’s examples.

### Cited Findings
- Example 2 at 89 FR 47036: a symptom tracker is “not a personal health record to the extent the site does not have the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources (i.e., if the consumer is its only source of information).” — [89 FR 47036](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- The wearable example treats the tracker as a second source of identifiable health information. The calendar-API example treats non-health data from another system as a second source. The geolocation-API and data-broker examples do the same. Capacity counts “even if some users elect not to connect.” An integration that “exists and that is able to draw” counts even if unused, unpublicized, or in beta. Capacity that would exist only after a code change the developer has not made does not. — [89 FR 47036–47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- At least one source must supply PHR identifiable health information. Health information the individual provides, and information created or received by a covered health care provider, health plan, employer, or clearinghouse, is the kind of information the definition covers. The app or device can itself be the “covered health care provider” if it furnishes health care services or supplies, which the symptom-tracker example treats as receiving the consumer’s inputs. — [16 CFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47036](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- The 2009 commentary treats data processing through service providers, consistent with disclosures and reasonable expectations, as use within the individual’s authorization, not as a separate source the record is drawn from. — [74 FR 42967–42968](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule).

### Inferences
- Family-loaded only, no provider, pharmacy, or other-app sync, and no implemented second feed: unlikely to be a PHR. This is the FTC’s Example 2. Records about a teenager do not change that test; nothing in Part 318 creates a separate minor-coverage trigger.
- Google Drive: a manual flow in which the family downloads a file and loads it is still the consumer as the only source. An implemented Drive API or import that pulls files into the record is a second source under the calendar-API analogy, including if the family never enables backup or import. Client-side-encrypted export to Drive is an outbound backup, not a source the record is drawn from. It matters to the “unsecured” question, not to whether a PHR exists.
- Hospital portal export or lab PDF: the hospital or lab may have created PHR identifiable health information, so the content element can be met, but the source the device draws from is the user if the user is the one who hands over the file. A portal or lab integration that the software can query is a second source even if unused.
- Apple Health (or any health-app or wearable sync): this is the diet-app / fitness-tracker example. Implemented sync capacity makes it a PHR even if this family does not connect it.
- User plus an AI model only: the model, on the facts given, reads prompts and returns checked answers. That is processing of information already in the record. It is not a second source in any example the Commission gave. It would be a closer question, and an unresolved one, if the product wrote model-generated health facts back into the record as a distinct feed. Sending the underlying record, or a checked answer that is itself identifiable health information, to ChatGPT or Claude can raise an authorization question, but only after the record is a PHR. The 2009 commentary would treat processing needed to provide the service the person asked for as authorized when it matches disclosures and expectations.

### Gaps
- No FTC example addresses a user-controlled file import from generic cloud storage, a PDF parser, or a model that writes inferences back into the record.
- Whether “internet-connected device” plus “other health-related services or tools” covers a storage vault that answers questions, as opposed to a tracker of symptoms, fitness, medications, or similar, is a fact-intensive reading of the catch-all. The Commission said the catch-all is limited by the tracking list’s context and that the offering must be more than tangential to health. A family health-record store is more than tangential. It is still not a PHR without multiple-source capacity.

## Is the maker a vendor, and are NEAR AI and Google service providers?

### Takeaway
The maker is a vendor only if it offers or maintains a personal health record. Publishing open-source software that a family runs, without hosting the record, is a weak fit for the Commission’s “in the business of offering or maintaining” gloss, and there is no PHR at all without multiple-source capacity. A future product the maker sells or hosts is much closer to a vendor if that product has the technical capacity to draw from more than one source. NEAR AI Cloud is a third party service provider, not a vendor and not a PHR related entity, only if a vendor exists and NEAR holds or uses unsecured PHR identifiable health information. Google, holding only client-side ciphertext, is likely outside the service-provider definition if that ciphertext meets the HHS method for “secured” data.

### Cited Findings
- Vendor status is “offers or maintains a personal health record,” excluding HIPAA-covered entities and business associates to the extent of those activities. The preamble: entities “not in the business of offering or maintaining (e.g., selling, marketing, providing, or promoting) a health-related product or service are not covered.” The duty in 16 CFR 318.3(a) is triggered for a vendor by a breach of unsecured PHR identifiable health information “that is in a personal health record maintained or offered by such vendor.” — [16 CFR 318.2 and 318.3(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47035](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- A third party service provider’s duty is not to notify individuals, the FTC, or the media. It notifies “an official designated in a written contract” by the vendor or PHR related entity, or if none is designated “a senior official,” obtains acknowledgment, and identifies each customer of that vendor or entity whose unsecured PHR identifiable health information was, or is reasonably believed to have been, acquired. Vendors and PHR related entities “shall notify third party service providers of their status.” The 2009 preamble added that sentence because a cloud provider may offer computing and storage “without knowing whether customers use them to offer PHRs.” Access in the course of providing services does not make the service provider a PHR related entity. — [16 CFR 318.3(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [74 FR 42970](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule).
- The service-provider definition requires that the entity hold, use, or disclose unsecured PHR identifiable health information as a result of the services. Information protected by the HHS technology specified under 42 U.S.C. 17932(h)(2) is not unsecured, and the rule does not apply to it. — [16 CFR 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- PHR related-entity prong (3) is an entity that accesses unsecured PHR identifiable health information in a PHR or sends it to a PHR. The Commission said cloud, analytics, and similar firms should be service providers, with notice running to the firm the consumer knows, not related entities. — [89 FR 47043–47044](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).

### Inferences
- Hackathon maker: the family maintains the record on hardware the family runs. The maker ships software and does not operate a cloud of unencrypted health records. Combined with the “in the business” gloss, that is a poor fit for “vendor.” “Providing” software is the word in the preamble that creates the residual uncertainty, not “maintains.”
- Future paid or hosted product: selling, marketing, or hosting a consumer health-record product is what the preamble describes as being in the business. Hosting is “maintains.” Vendor status still requires that the thing offered be a personal health record, so a paid app that remains consumer-only, with no second-source capacity, is still Example 2.
- NEAR AI Cloud: if the maker is a vendor, and prompts processed inside the TEE contain unsecured PHR identifiable health information, NEAR matches the cloud-processor example of a third party service provider. Its duty would be notice to the vendor, not to the family or the FTC. The rule says that access does not convert it into a PHR related entity. If the maker is not a vendor and the product is not a PHR, the service-provider duty, which runs to a vendor or PHR related entity, does not independently appear. Whether plaintext inside a TEE is “unsecured” is unresolved; the regulatory safe harbor is the HHS method, which the preamble describes as specified encryption, not confidential computing.
- Google Drive: ciphertext the user encrypts before upload, with the key kept by the family, is the fact pattern the “secured” definition is aimed at, if the encryption is the HHS-specified method. A provider that only stores that ciphertext is not, on the text, holding unsecured PHR identifiable health information. That conclusion depends on the encryption actually meeting the HHS guidance. Google is not the vendor of this record.

### Gaps
- No opinion, rulemaking example, or case found decides whether an open-source publisher that never possesses the data “offers” a PHR.
- No source found decides whether TEE processing is “secured” under the HHS guidance incorporated at 16 CFR 318.2.
- Contractual designation of a notice official, which 318.3(b) contemplates, is a design fact, not a coverage fact. It was not researched against the NEAR or Google terms.

## What the rule requires if it applies, and which cases are HBNR cases

### Takeaway
If it applies, a vendor or PHR related entity notifies affected U.S. individuals, the FTC, and, at 500 or more residents of a state or jurisdiction, prominent media. Individual and media notice, and the service provider’s notice to the vendor, are due without unreasonable delay and within 60 calendar days of discovery. For 500 or more individuals, FTC notice is contemporaneous with individual notice. For fewer than 500, the FTC log may be annual. A disclosure the privacy policy does not allow can be a breach without a hack. GoodRx (2023) and Easy Healthcare / Premom (2023) are HBNR cases that also alleged FTC Act section 5. BetterHelp (2023) is a section 5 case, not an HBNR civil-penalty case. No later HBNR complaint was found.

### Cited Findings
- 16 CFR 318.3(a): after discovery, the vendor (for information in a PHR it maintains or offers) and the PHR related entity (for information obtained through its product or service) shall notify (1) each affected U.S. citizen or resident, (2) the FTC, and (3) “prominent media outlets serving a State or jurisdiction” if unsecured PHR identifiable health information of 500 or more residents of that state or jurisdiction is, or is reasonably believed to have been, acquired. — [eCFR 318.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- Discovery, 16 CFR 318.3(c): the breach is discovered on the first day it is known or reasonably should have been known to the vendor, PHR related entity, or third party service provider. Knowledge of an employee, officer, or agent, other than the person committing the breach, is imputed. — [eCFR 318.3(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- Timing, 16 CFR 318.4. Individual notice, media notice, and the service provider’s notice to the vendor: “without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after the discovery of a breach of security,” subject to a law-enforcement delay implemented like 45 CFR 164.528(a)(2). FTC notice for 500 or more individuals: “contemporaneously” with that individual notice, so also no later than 60 calendar days. FTC notice for fewer than 500: the entity “may” log the breach and submit the log “annually … no later than 60 calendar days following the end of the calendar year.” The entity has the burden of proving notice was made, including any delay. — [eCFR 318.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- The 2009 rule already used 60 calendar days for notice to individuals. For a breach affecting 500 or more individuals, notice to the FTC was “as soon as possible and in no case later than ten business days” after discovery. The 2024 preamble replaced that ten-business-day FTC deadline with contemporaneous notice, because ten business days often produced incomplete notices. — [89 FR 47028 preamble, including the discussion adopting revised 318.4(b)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- Method, 16 CFR 318.5. Individual notice is written notice at the last known address. Electronic mail may be used if the individual specified it as the primary method, and electronic-mail notice must be clear and conspicuous. “Electronic mail means email in combination with one or more of the following: text message, within-application messaging, or electronic banner.” If email is not available or was not specified, first-class mail may be used. If the individual is deceased, notice goes to next of kin when the individual provided contact information and authorization to contact them. If contact information for ten or more people is insufficient or out of date, substitute notice is a 90-day conspicuous home-page posting or notice in major print or broadcast media where the people likely live, plus a toll-free number active at least 90 days. Urgent cases may add telephone or other means. FTC notice follows instructions on the FTC website. — [eCFR 318.2 and 318.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- Content, 16 CFR 318.6, plain language, to the extent possible: (a) what happened, including the date of the breach and of discovery if known, and the name or identity of third parties that acquired the information, or a description if naming them would pose a risk; (b) types of unsecured PHR identifiable health information involved, with examples that include full name, Social Security number, date of birth, home address, account number, diagnosis, lab results, medications, other treatment information, use of a health-related mobile application, or device identifier in combination with another data element; (c) steps individuals should take; (d) what the entity is doing to investigate, mitigate, protect against further breaches, and protect individuals, “such as offering credit monitoring or other services”; (e) contact procedures including two or more of a toll-free telephone number, email address, website, within-application messaging, or postal address. — [eCFR 318.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- GoodRx is an HBNR case and a section 5 case. The Commission’s first HBNR enforcement action was announced February 1, 2023. The complaint alleged GoodRx, as a vendor of personal health records, disclosed users’ prescription medications, health conditions, contact information, and advertising identifiers to Facebook, Google, and other advertising platforms, contrary to privacy promises, and did not give the HBNR notices. The settlement included a $1.5 million civil penalty and conduct relief, including a ban on disclosing health information to advertisers for most advertising purposes. The 2024 preamble restates that charging theory. The section 5 counts included deception (privacy promises) and unfairness (sharing for advertising without affirmative express consent, and inadequate privacy procedures). — [89 FR 47028 preamble](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule); [WilmerHale, February 3, 2023](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230203-ftc-brings-first-of-its-kind-enforcement-action-against-goodrx-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2023/02/ftc-seeks-firstever-health-breach-notification-rule-enforcement); [Thompson Coburn](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/ftc-issues-fine-to-goodrx-over-information-sharing-102jc70/).
- Easy Healthcare, developer of the Premom fertility app, is the second HBNR action, announced May 17, 2023, and it also alleged section 5. The FTC alleged SDK sharing of health information contrary to representations and without the HBNR notices. The order included a $100,000 civil penalty for the HBNR violation, a ban on sharing health information with third parties for advertising, affirmative-express-consent requirements for other sharing, deletion requests to recipients, and consumer notice. Separate $100,000 payments to Connecticut, the District of Columbia, and Oregon were reported for state-law claims. — [WilmerHale, May 25, 2023](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230525-ftc-brings-second-enforcement-action-against-healthcare-company-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule); [Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/ftc-announces-second-enforcement-action-under-health-breach-notification-rule-against-fertility-app-developer-easy-healthcare/); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/06/ftc-settles-with-premom-app-developer).
- BetterHelp is a section 5 case, not an HBNR civil-penalty case. Proskauer (March 2023) describes a March 2, 2023 settlement: $7.8 million in partial refunds to customers, a first FTC consumer-redress order of that kind, for sharing mental-health intake information with advertising platforms contrary to privacy statements and for displaying seals implying HIPAA compliance. Proskauer expressly contrasts that section 5 redress action with GoodRx’s HBNR civil penalty a month earlier. — [Proskauer](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2023/03/articles/data-privacy-laws/ftcs-one-two-punch-on-data-tracking-and-health-privacy/). Davis Wright Tremaine’s May 2024 post groups BetterHelp with GoodRx as part of “a series of recent enforcement actions” about tracking pixels and then discusses the HBNR final rule. That grouping does not make BetterHelp an HBNR count; the money Proskauer describes is consumer redress under section 5, not an HBNR civil penalty. — [DWT](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/05/ftc-finalizes-hbnr-to-cover-health-app-breaches).
- Searches for a 2024, 2025, or 2026 HBNR complaint or civil penalty other than the 2026 policy-statement rescission and the 2023 cases did not return a later HBNR enforcement action.

### Inferences
- On the design described, a checked answer shown to an assistant the family chose to query is the kind of processing the 2009 commentary treats as authorized when it is consistent with what the family was told. A disclosure of the underlying record, or of health details the privacy policy says will not leave the device, to a company that is not permitted to have them, is the GoodRx theory of breach: no hack required. That theory applies only if a covered PHR and a covered vendor or related entity exist.
- SSNs, tax forms, and passports stored beside health records are not independently PHR identifiable health information under the 2009 name-and-credit-card discussion. Section 318.6(b) lists Social Security number as an example of a data type to describe if it was involved in a breach of unsecured PHR identifiable health information. An incident confined to passports or tax forms is not, by itself, an HBNR breach. Other breach laws may be; they are outside this note.
- The rule does not contain a special notice path for a teenager’s record. Notice runs to the individual whose information was acquired. The only next-of-kin provision found is for a deceased individual who supplied contact information and authorization.

### Gaps
- Case numbers and the filed complaints were not opened. Penalty and charge descriptions rest on the 2024 preamble and law-firm accounts of the settlements.
- No later HBNR complaint was found. That is not a complete FTC docket review through November 2026.
- The live FTC breach-notice form and the public list of 500-plus breaches were not opened. Section 318.5(c) points filers to “instructions at the Federal Trade Commission’s website.”

## Penalties

### Takeaway
A violation of Part 318 is treated as a violation of a rule under section 18 of the FTC Act, so civil penalties are available. The inflation-adjusted maximum for a knowing violation of such a rule, FTC Act section 5(m)(1)(A), is $53,088. That figure took effect January 17, 2025. The FTC left it unchanged for 2026.

### Cited Findings
- 16 CFR 318.7: “Any violation of this part shall be treated as a violation of a rule promulgated under section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, 15 U.S.C. 57a, regarding unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and thus subject to civil penalties (as adjusted for inflation pursuant to § 1.98 of this chapter),” and the Commission enforces it with the powers available under the FTC Act. The 2024 preamble describes this section as articulating penalties that the Recovery Act already attached. — [eCFR 318.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- FTC inflation adjustment published January 17, 2025, document 2025-01361, effective January 17, 2025. Section 5(m)(1)(A) of the FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(A), “knowing violation of a rule respecting unfair or deceptive acts or practices,” increased from $51,744 to $53,088. The same notice increased section 5(l) order-violation penalties and section 5(m)(1)(B) penalties to $53,088. Adjustments apply to penalties assessed after the effective date, including where the violation predated the adjustment. — [90 FR adjustment notice, January 17, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/17/2025-01361/adjustments-to-civil-penalty-amounts).
- FTC notice published September 15, 2026, document 2026-18853, effective September 15, 2026: “the Federal Trade Commission (‘FTC’)’s civil penalty amounts will remain unchanged during 2026. The FTC will continue to apply the 2025 civil penalty levels.” The notice relies on OMB Memorandum M-26-11 (April 17, 2026), which cancelled the 2026 inflation adjustment because the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not produce the October 2025 CPI-U during a government shutdown, so there was no cost-of-living multiplier. — [Federal Register, September 15, 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/15/2026-18853/civil-penalty-inflation-adjustments).
- Settled HBNR amounts were far below a mechanical maximum times the number of people: GoodRx $1.5 million; Easy Healthcare $100,000 for the HBNR count. Those are negotiated civil penalties, not a holding about how to count violations. — [WilmerHale on GoodRx](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230203-ftc-brings-first-of-its-kind-enforcement-action-against-goodrx-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule); [WilmerHale on Easy Healthcare](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230525-ftc-brings-second-enforcement-action-against-healthcare-company-for-violating-the-health-breach-notification-rule).

### Inferences
- The figure to cite as of November 8, 2026 is $53,088 per violation under section 5(m)(1)(A), as adjusted January 17, 2025 and expressly continued for 2026. Section 5(m)(1)(A) is the penalty for a knowing violation of a section 18 rule, which is how 318.7 classifies a violation of Part 318. This note does not resolve what counts as one “violation” (a person, a day, a disclosure). The statute’s knowledge requirement is outside the inflation notice’s dollar figure; the notice labels the provision as a “knowing violation.”

### Gaps
- The Federal Register volume and page for document 2026-18853 were not confirmed beyond the document number, publication date, and the search snippet’s printed-page reference. The URL and the “remain unchanged” sentence were retrieved.
- 16 CFR 1.98’s current table was not separately opened. The January 17, 2025 adjustment notice is the source for the $53,088 figure.

## Interaction with HIPAA

### Takeaway
The rule exempts HIPAA-covered entities, and any other entity to the extent it is acting as a business associate of a HIPAA-covered entity. A consumer health record that is not a covered entity and is not acting as a business associate can still be under the FTC rule. That is the reason section 13407 exists.

### Cited Findings
- 16 CFR 318.1(a): “This part does not apply to HIPAA-covered entities, or to any other entity to the extent that it engages in activities as a business associate of a HIPAA-covered entity.” “HIPAA-covered entity” and “business associate” take their meanings from 45 CFR 160.103. The same carve-out is repeated inside the definitions of vendor and PHR related entity. — [eCFR 318.1 and 318.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).
- The 2024 preamble: Congress enacted section 13407 because vendors of personal health records and related entities “were collecting consumers’ health information but were not subject to the privacy and security requirements of HIPAA,” and directed the FTC to require those “non-HIPAA covered entities, and their third party service providers,” to notify. “The Rule does not apply to businesses or organizations covered by HIPAA. HIPAA-covered entities and their business associates must instead comply with HHS’s breach notification rule.” The Commission declined to put a second HIPAA exemption inside the PHR definition because 318.1 already does that work. — [89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- The 2009 preamble describes a HIPAA-covered entity, citing 45 CFR 160.103, as a health care provider that conducts certain transactions electronically, a health care clearinghouse, or a health plan, and a business associate as an entity that handles protected health information for such a covered entity. — [74 FR 42962](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/08/25/E9-20142/health-breach-notification-rule).

### Inferences
- A household device the family runs, and a software maker that is not a provider, plan, or clearinghouse conducting HIPAA transactions and is not performing services for one, sits on the FTC side of the line if the PHR and vendor tests are met. Not being a HIPAA covered entity is the start of the HBNR question, not the end of it. The HIPAA analysis itself is out of scope here.

### Gaps
- No determination is made about whether a future hosted service could become a business associate by contracting with a hospital, plan, or other covered entity. The product facts given do not describe that relationship.

## Applicability call

### Takeaway
(a) Hackathon, self-hosted software the family runs: unlikely. (b) A future consumer product: depends, and becomes likely if the company is in the business of offering or hosting the record and the software has an implemented ability to draw from a second source. The two facts that flip either version are multiple-source technical capacity and whether the maker, rather than only the family, offers or maintains the record.

### Cited Findings
- Not a PHR “if the consumer is its only source of information,” even when the operator receives identifiable symptom information. Implemented capacity to draw from a wearable, a data broker, a geolocation API, or a calendar API is enough, including unused capacity. Capacity that requires code the developer has not written is not enough. — [89 FR 47036–47038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- A vendor is an entity in the business of offering or maintaining (selling, marketing, providing, or promoting) a personal health record whose offering relates more than tangentially to health, and the notice duty attaches to a record “maintained or offered by such vendor.” — [16 CFR 318.2 and 318.3(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318); [89 FR 47035](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule).
- The September 9, 2026 withdrawal of the 2021 policy statement, as Hunton and the AHA describe it, left the 2024 rule in place. The eCFR source note on November 8, 2026 still ends at the May 30, 2024 amendment. — [Hunton, September 16, 2026](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-rescinds-2021-policy-statement-on-health-app-data-breaches); [AHA, September 10, 2026](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-09-10-ftc-rescinds-policy-statement-extending-breach-notification-rule-apps-devices-collect-health); [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318).

### Inferences
- (a) Unlikely for the current hackathon design. The family loads the records, the family runs the device, and the maker does not operate a store of unencrypted health records. That is Example 2 plus a weak vendor fact. It flips toward coverage if the software as shipped can import or sync from a second system (Apple Health, a hospital or lab portal, a Google Drive API that pulls files in, a wearable, a calendar or geolocation API), even if this family never turns that feature on. It does not flip merely because a hospital or lab originally wrote a PDF the family uploads, or because a TEE model answers questions, or because Drive holds client-side ciphertext. It also flips if the maker, not the family, maintains the record.
- (b) Depends for a future consumer product. Likely if the company sells, markets, or hosts a consumer health-record product and that product has implemented second-source capacity. Still unlikely, under Example 2, if the paid product remains a local vault whose only source is the user and the company never maintains the records. “Paid” alone does not create a PHR. Hosting unencrypted health records, or shipping a sync integration, does.
- NEAR AI and Google do not pull the hackathon build into the rule by themselves. Their duties are derivative of a vendor or PHR related entity. Google-as-ciphertext-store is, if the encryption matches the HHS method, outside “unsecured.” NEAR-as-TEE-processor is a service-provider question that arises only after vendor coverage is established, and the TEE-versus-encryption point is unverified.

### Gaps
- This call is an orientation from the regulation and the 2024 preamble examples. It is not a determination that the FTC would decline a case, and it is not legal advice.
- Epstein Becker’s September 2026 article, if a reader treats it as controlling, would push both (a) and (b) further toward “unlikely.” The codified rule and the Hunton/AHA accounts of the same FTC action do not support that reading. Until the FTC’s own September 9, 2026 document is in hand, that disagreement should stay visible.
