# HIPAA covered-entity and business-associate status for a household health-records device

Orientation research for one product, current as of November 8, 2026. This is not legal advice. Conclusions below are labeled likely, unlikely, or depends. The eCFR text cited here is the continuously updated, unofficial codification, retrieved November 8, 2026. Official amendment history is in the Federal Register.

Product facts used, and no others: an open-source household device (Raspberry Pi), built as a US hackathon project, with a possible later consumer product. The family operates it. It stores the family's own SSNs, tax forms, passports, and health records, including a teenager's records. It answers questions from consumer AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude) by running a confidential-computing cloud model (NEAR AI Cloud, a trusted execution environment) and checks every answer before the assistant sees it. It may import from, and back up to, Google Drive with client-side encryption. The software maker distributes open-source software and might later sell a consumer product. The working assumption is that the maker does not provide medical care, insurance, or billing, and does not receive the family's unencrypted health records in the ordinary design. Where that assumption changes the answer, it is flagged.

No circuit split was found, in the sources reviewed, on whether a household or a direct-to-consumer personal health record is a HIPAA covered entity. The live 2024–2026 disputes are about already-regulated entities (a vacated reproductive-health Privacy Rule amendment, and a still-proposed Security Rule update). They do not pull this self-hosted family device into HIPAA.

## Who is a HIPAA covered entity?

### Takeaway
Likely answer, and it is stable: a covered entity is only a health plan, a health care clearinghouse, or a health care provider that transmits health information electronically in connection with a standard HIPAA transaction. Holding health-related files is not the test. On the stated facts, neither the family nor the software maker is likely a covered entity.

### Cited Findings
- Statute, Social Security Act § 1172, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1(a): any standard adopted under that part applies to (1) a health plan, (2) a health care clearinghouse, and (3) a health care provider who transmits any health information in electronic form in connection with a transaction referred to in 42 U.S.C. § 1320d-2(a)(1). Cornell's prior-provision notes identify § 1320d as the successor to SSA § 1171 and § 1320d-1 as the successor to SSA § 1172. HIPAA's administrative-simplification purpose is stated in Pub. L. 104-191, title II, § 261. — [42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d-1); [42 U.S.C. § 1320d](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- Statutory definitions at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d (SSA § 1171): a health care provider includes a Medicare provider of services, a provider of medical or other health services, and any other person furnishing health care services or supplies. A health care clearinghouse processes or facilitates processing nonstandard health-information data elements into standard data elements. A health plan is an individual or group plan that provides, or pays the cost of, medical care. A group health plan counts only if it has 50 or more participants or is administered by an entity other than the employer that established and maintains it. "Health information" and "individually identifiable health information" must be created or received by a listed institution (a provider, plan, public health authority, employer, life insurer, school or university, or clearinghouse for health information; a provider, plan, employer, or clearinghouse for individually identifiable health information) and must relate to health, care, or payment. — [42 U.S.C. § 1320d](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- Regulation, 45 C.F.R. § 160.103: "Covered entity means: (1) A health plan. (2) A health care clearinghouse. (3) A health care provider who transmits any health information in electronic form in connection with a transaction covered by this subchapter." Same section: a health care provider is a Medicare provider of services, a provider of medical or health services, or any other person or organization who furnishes, bills, or is paid for health care in the normal course of business. A clearinghouse converts between nonstandard and standard data or transactions. A transaction is a transmission between two parties to carry out financial or administrative activities related to health care, including claims, remittance, coordination of benefits, claim status, enrollment, eligibility, premium payments, referral certification and authorization, first report of injury, claims attachments, and electronic funds transfer. — [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103) (eCFR, accessed November 8, 2026)
- 45 C.F.R. § 160.102(a)–(b): the HIPAA subchapter applies to health plans, clearinghouses, and providers who transmit health information electronically in connection with a covered transaction, and, where provided, to a business associate. Source note: amended at 78 Fed. Reg. 5687 (Jan. 25, 2013) to extend specified standards to business associates. — [45 C.F.R. § 160.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/section-160.102) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- 45 C.F.R. § 164.104 mirrors § 160.102 for the Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules in Part 164, and was amended at 78 Fed. Reg. 5692 (Jan. 25, 2013). — [45 C.F.R. Part 164](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- HHS states the same three categories in plain language and adds: "If an entity does not meet the definition of a covered entity or business associate, it does not have to comply with the HIPAA Rules." — [Covered Entities and Business Associates, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/index.html) (page text retrieved November 8, 2026; no separate "last reviewed" date captured)
- CMS describes the same three categories and the same electronic-transaction limit for providers. — [Are You a Covered Entity?, CMS](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/burden-reduction/administrative-simplification/hipaa/covered-entities) (retrieved November 8, 2026)
- HITECH (Pub. L. 111-5, §§ 13400–13424) did not add families, app makers, or personal-health-record vendors to the covered-entity list. It made business associates directly liable for specified requirements and required certain vendors that serve covered entities to be treated as business associates. HITECH § 13400 defines "covered entity" by cross-reference to 45 C.F.R. § 160.103. — enrolled HITECH text as reproduced by EPIC, [H.R. 1 Subtitle D](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/pdf/StimulusPassedBill-SubD.pdf); implemented in the Omnibus Rule, [78 Fed. Reg. 5566 (Jan. 25, 2013)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)

### Inferences
- Unlikely for this product. The family stores its own records. It does not, on the stated facts, furnish, bill, or get paid for health care in the normal course of business, operate a health plan, or convert claims into HIPAA standard transactions. The maker is assumed not to provide care, insurance, or billing. Distributing software, including software that can hold health records, is not one of the three covered-entity categories.
- The fact that would flip covered-entity status is not "the files are sensitive." It is role. A family member who is a provider and uses the device to transmit a standard transaction (electronic claims, eligibility, and the like), or a maker that later operates a clinic or a health plan, would be analyzed separately. That is not this product's stated design.
- "PHI-like" is not PHI. Protected health information is individually identifiable health information, in a listed medium, that is in the hands of a covered entity or business associate, with exclusions for education records, certain education-treatment records, employment records held by a covered entity in its role as employer, and people dead more than 50 years. See the definition in the next section. Data that never enters that legal category is not regulated by the HIPAA Rules even if it is a medical record in ordinary speech.

### Gaps
- Cornell's section notes were used for the SSA § 1171 / § 1172 crosswalk. The rest of the SSA § 1173–1179 numbering was not re-checked section by section against the official U.S. Code in this pass. The operative applicability text is § 1320d-1, which was fetched.
- No court opinion was found in this pass holding that a household, or a hackathon device maker, is a covered entity. Absence of cases is noted, not treated as a holding.

## Is a family storing its own records a covered entity, and is a personal health record or consumer app a covered entity just because it holds PHI-like data?

### Takeaway
Unlikely. A family is not a covered entity by storing its own health records, including a teenager's records. A personal health record or consumer app is not a covered entity merely because the data look like a medical chart. HHS's own app scenarios say a developer that only serves the consumer is not likely a covered entity or a business associate.

### Cited Findings
- Covered-entity status is the three-category test in 45 C.F.R. § 160.103 and 42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1(a), quoted in the previous section. There is no fourth category for individuals, families, or consumer apps. — [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103); [42 U.S.C. § 1320d-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d-1)
- "Protected health information" means individually identifiable health information transmitted or maintained in electronic media or any other form, excluding education records covered by FERPA, records described at 20 U.S.C. § 1232g(a)(4)(B)(iv), employment records held by a covered entity in its role as employer, and information about a person deceased more than 50 years. Individually identifiable health information must itself have been created or received by a health care provider, health plan, employer, or health care clearinghouse. — [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- HHS cloud guidance uses the same boundary in plain language: individually identifiable health information is "called protected health information or PHI when created, received, maintained, or transmitted by a HIPAA covered entity or business associate." — [Guidance on HIPAA & Cloud Computing, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html) (content last reviewed December 23, 2022; page retrieved November 8, 2026)
- OCR, February 2016 scenarios (the PDF is undated on its face; a law-firm writeup dates the scenarios to February 2016): "Only health plans, health care clearinghouses and most health care providers are covered entities under HIPAA." A consumer who downloads an app and enters her own glucose and blood-pressure readings is not dealing with a business associate, because the developer is not creating, receiving, maintaining, or transmitting PHI on behalf of a covered entity. The same answer holds if she downloads records from a patient portal and uploads them herself. OCR's closing test: "If you are only offering services directly to and collecting information for or on behalf of consumers, and not on behalf a provider, health plan or health care clearinghouse, you are not likely to be subject to HIPAA as either a covered entity or business associate." — [Health App Use Scenarios & HIPAA (HHS PDF)](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-health-app-developer-scenarios-2-2016.pdf); date corroborated by [Haynes and Boone, May 4, 2016](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/publications/ocr-hipaa-guidance-for-mobile-health-developers.pdf)
- HITECH § 13400 defines a personal health record as an electronic record of PHR identifiable health information, drawn from multiple sources, managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual, and defines a vendor of personal health records as an entity other than a covered entity that offers or maintains a personal health record. The separate breach-notice duty for those vendors is titled for "vendors of personal health records and other non-HIPAA covered entities" and runs to the FTC, not through the HIPAA Privacy Rule. — [HITECH enrolled text, §§ 13400 and 13407](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/pdf/StimulusPassedBill-SubD.pdf)
- 2013 Omnibus preamble: a personal-health-record vendor may offer records directly to individuals and also offer them on behalf of covered entities. "In such cases, the personal health record vendor is only subject to HIPAA as a business associate with respect to personal health records that are offered to individuals on behalf of covered entities." Offering a record directly, or merely having an interoperability link the individual authorizes, does not by itself make the vendor a business associate. — [78 Fed. Reg. 5566, 5571–72 (Jan. 25, 2013)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- A business associate "is only subject to the HIPAA Rules with respect to the protected health information it maintains, uses, or discloses on behalf of a covered entity (or business associate) and not to other information it may maintain, including health information." — same Omnibus preamble, 78 Fed. Reg. at 5572 area (response on hybrid entities / health care components), [78 Fed. Reg. 5566](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- Norton Rose Fulbright, in a February 27, 2026 international practice guide, still describes the same split: HIPAA applies to healthcare entities; the FTC oversees non-HIPAA health data, including consumer health apps; state comprehensive privacy laws can cover health data outside HIPAA. — [ICLG Digital Health Laws and Regulations 2026, USA, Norton Rose Fulbright, published February 27, 2026](https://iclg.com/practice-areas/digital-health-laws-and-regulations/usa/)

### Inferences
- Unlikely that the family is a covered entity. Operating a box in the house that holds copies of medical records is not furnishing care, running a plan, or running a clearinghouse. Copies of clinical records were health information when a provider created them. Once the family holds them outside a covered entity or business associate, they are not PHI in the HIPAA sense, which is why OCR tells app developers that consumer-entered and consumer-uploaded charts do not make the developer regulated.
- Unlikely that the maker is a covered entity or a personal-health-record vendor "covered by HIPAA" merely because the device can store health records. HITECH defines a personal-health-record vendor as someone other than a covered entity. That definition is a reason HIPAA does not apply, not a reason it does. Whether the health-record function is a "personal health record" for the FTC's separate breach rule is a different statute and was not analyzed here.
- The teenager's records do not flip covered-entity status. HIPAA's minor rules bind covered entities: a covered entity treats a parent as the personal representative of an unemancipated minor, with exceptions when the minor consents to care and other consent is not required. 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(g). Those duties never start if no covered entity holds the record. State minor-consent and parental-access laws can still limit how the family got the records and who in the household may see them. That is outside HIPAA. This note did not survey state minor-consent statutes.
- A symptom diary the family writes itself may not even be "health information" under 42 U.S.C. § 1320d(4), because that definition requires creation or receipt by a provider, plan, public health authority, employer, life insurer, school, or clearinghouse. A downloaded clinic record does meet the "created by a provider" piece and still is not PHI unless a covered entity or business associate holds or transmits it.

### Gaps
- No HHS FAQ was found that uses the words "family" or "household device." The conclusion is the application of the covered-entity definition and the 2016 consumer-app scenarios, not a document about this product.
- State law on a parent's access to a teenager's records was not researched.

## When is a software vendor a business associate?

### Takeaway
Depends on whose behalf the vendor acts, and on whether it creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI for that entity. (a) Software the family runs itself, with no vendor access, is unlikely to make the maker a business associate. (b) A vendor that hosts or can access electronic PHI for a covered entity is likely a business associate, and encryption or a trusted execution environment does not remove that status if the vendor maintains the data for the covered entity. (c) A vendor that only sells software to consumers, or even to a covered entity, is unlikely a business associate if it never has access to PHI. (d) A personal-health-record vendor that offers the record to individuals, not on behalf of a covered entity, is unlikely a business associate. The regulatory text does not use the word "conduit." The conduit idea is a narrow HHS interpretation: mere couriers that only transmit, plus temporary storage incident to transmission, are not business associates. Persistent storage is not a conduit, even if the vendor never looks at the data.

### Cited Findings
- 45 C.F.R. § 160.103, business associate, paragraph (1): with respect to a covered entity, a person who, other than as workforce, (i) on behalf of the covered entity or its organized health care arrangement, creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information for a function or activity regulated by the subchapter (claims, data analysis, utilization review, quality assurance, patient safety activities, billing, benefit management, practice management, repricing), or (ii) provides legal, actuarial, accounting, consulting, data aggregation, management, administrative, accreditation, or financial services where the service involves disclosure of PHI from the covered entity or arrangement, or from another business associate. — [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- Paragraph (3) expressly includes: (i) a Health Information Organization, E-prescribing Gateway, or other person that provides data transmission services with respect to PHI to a covered entity and that requires access on a routine basis; (ii) a person that offers a personal health record to one or more individuals on behalf of a covered entity; (iii) a subcontractor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of the business associate. Paragraph (4) excludes treating providers receiving information for treatment, plan sponsors meeting § 164.504(f), certain government eligibility agencies, and covered entities acting within an organized health care arrangement. — same section
- The "on behalf of a covered entity" personal-health-record clause was added by the Omnibus Rule to implement HITECH. HITECH § 13408 requires a written business-associate contract, and business-associate treatment, for an organization that transmits PHI to a covered entity and requires routine access (health information exchange, regional health information organization, e-prescribing gateway) and for "each vendor that contracts with a covered entity to allow that covered entity to offer a personal health record to patients as part of its electronic health record." The regulation's phrase is broader than the statutory example: "on behalf of a covered entity," not only "as part of its electronic health record." — [HITECH § 13408 enrolled text](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/pdf/StimulusPassedBill-SubD.pdf); [78 Fed. Reg. 5566, 5571 (Jan. 25, 2013)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- Omnibus preamble on conduits: the exception "is a narrow one and is intended to exclude only those entities providing mere courier services, such as the U.S. Postal Service or United Parcel Service and their electronic equivalents, such as internet service providers (ISPs) providing mere data transmission services." A conduit "transports information but does not access it other than on a random or infrequent basis." The exception "is limited to transmission services (whether digital or hard copy), including any temporary storage of transmitted data incident to such transmission." "In contrast, an entity that maintains protected health information on behalf of a covered entity is a business associate and not a conduit, even if the entity does not actually view the protected health information." The difference is "the transient versus persistent nature of that opportunity." Document-storage companies that maintain PHI are business associates even if they do not view it. A personal-health-record vendor operating a record on behalf of a covered entity is a business associate if it has access, "regardless of whether the personal health record vendor actually exercises this access." — [78 Fed. Reg. 5566, 5571–72 (Jan. 25, 2013)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- Same preamble: status follows the definition, not the paperwork. "A person becomes a business associate by definition, not by the act of contracting with a covered entity." Liability for impermissible uses and disclosures "attaches immediately when a person creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity or business associate and otherwise meets the definition." Direct-to-consumer records stay outside that relationship. An interoperability arrangement alone, under which the individual's authorization causes a record to be sent to a personal-health-record vendor, does not mean the vendor offers the record on behalf of the covered entity. — same Federal Register document
- HHS business-associate FAQ, content last reviewed October 21, 2026, repeats the rule and the examples: a personal-health-record vendor is a business associate when it offers the record to individuals on behalf of a covered entity; an app developer is one when it contracts with a covered entity for patient-management services involving PHI; a cloud service provider engaged to create, receive, maintain, or transmit electronic PHI on behalf of a covered entity or business associate is one; an IT or EHR vendor is one when maintenance or support requires it to create, receive, maintain, or transmit electronic PHI. The same page's 2026 examples include a "third-party vendor super intelligence (SI) chatbot on a provider's patient portal" that handles PHI for symptom assessment, reminders, or scheduling. Conduit language on that page: transmission through a person that acts only as a conduit, "for example, the US Postal Service, certain private couriers, and their electronic equivalents." "The conduit exception is limited to entities that only provide transmission services for PHI (whether in electronic or paper form), including any temporary storage of PHI. Entities that access PHI on a regular or frequent basis to perform a service on behalf of a covered entity are not conduits." — [Business Associates FAQs, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html) (content last reviewed October 21, 2026)
- HHS software-vendor FAQ: "The mere selling or providing of software to a covered entity does not give rise to a business associate relationship if the vendor does not have access to the protected health information of the covered entity." Hosting patient information on the vendor's server, or accessing it while troubleshooting, does. Created December 19, 2002; content last reviewed December 28, 2022. — [Is a software vendor a business associate of a covered entity?, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/is-software-vendor-business-associate/index.html)
- OCR cloud guidance: when a covered entity engages a cloud service provider to create, receive, maintain, or transmit electronic PHI on its behalf, the provider is a business associate. "This is true even if the CSP processes or stores only encrypted ePHI and lacks an encryption key for the data. Lacking an encryption key does not exempt a CSP from business associate status." An entity that maintains electronic PHI on behalf of a covered entity is a business associate "even if the entity cannot actually view the ePHI." OCR calls these "no-view services." Encryption reduces viewing risk and does not, by itself, satisfy confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The guidance also says a covered entity or business associate may use a cloud service if it has a business-associate agreement and otherwise complies. — [Guidance on HIPAA & Cloud Computing, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html) (content last reviewed December 23, 2022). Contemporaneous firm summary: [Mintz, October 19, 2016](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2016-10-19-hhs-publishes-guidance-hipaa-and-cloud-computing)
- 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(e): a covered entity may disclose PHI to a business associate, and may allow the business associate to create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on its behalf, only with satisfactory assurances, documented in a written contract meeting § 164.504(e). A business associate must get the same assurances before a subcontractor does that work. — [45 C.F.R. Part 164, § 164.502](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164) (accessed November 8, 2026)
- OCR 2016 app scenarios, mapped onto the four vendor postures: consumer downloads and fills the app, no; consumer uploads portal data herself, no; provider recommends an app and the consumer later sends a summary, no; consumer-requested interoperability, with the developer transmitting on the consumer's behalf, no. Provider contracts for patient-management services and the app writes into the EHR, yes. Health-plan-offered personal health record, yes for that version; the same developer's separate direct-to-consumer app, no, if the data are kept separate. — [Health App Use Scenarios & HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-health-app-developer-scenarios-2-2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- (a) Family runs the software, maker has no access. Unlikely a business associate. There is no covered entity in the chain, so there is no one to be a business associate of. Even if a clinic later installed the same open-source code on the clinic's own machines and the maker never received electronic PHI, the software-vendor FAQ says the sale or provision of software, without access, does not create the relationship. The ordinary-design assumption (maker does not receive unencrypted records) matters here only once a covered entity is the customer. It is not what keeps the family outside HIPAA.
- The assumption does not do the work for the family-only design. If the maker could read the family's unencrypted records, the maker would still be unlikely a business associate, because it would be receiving the consumer's information, not acting on behalf of a covered entity. That is the direct-to-consumer half of OCR's sixth scenario and the Omnibus "only subject to HIPAA" sentence. The assumption matters a great deal for a later covered-entity deployment: access, hosting, or support that requires the records makes the maker a business associate even without a signed contract, because status follows the definition.
- (b) Vendor hosts or can access electronic PHI for a covered entity. Likely a business associate, including a subcontractor of a business associate. Client-side encryption and a trusted execution environment are the "no-view" fact pattern if the vendor still maintains or processes the data for the covered entity. HHS rejected the argument that inability to see plaintext avoids the status. The conduit exception is a poor fit for storage or for a model that computes over the data. Conduit is the postal service and an ISP that only carries packets, with random or infrequent access, plus temporary storage incident to transmission.
- Applied to Google Drive and NEAR AI Cloud under the stated facts: while the customer is the family, neither is likely a HIPAA business associate, because the family is not a covered entity. If a covered entity later used the same backup or the same confidential-computing model to handle electronic PHI, Google (persistent encrypted storage) is likely a business associate under the no-view guidance, not a conduit. NEAR is likely a business associate or subcontractor if it receives or processes that electronic PHI for the covered entity or for a business-associate maker, even inside a trusted execution environment. No HHS page reviewed uses the words "trusted execution environment." The no-view cloud guidance is the closest official analogue. Uncertainty remains if the cloud truly only transports data transiently and does not maintain it. OCR calls that exception narrow and fact-specific. Processing a prompt to produce an answer is a service, not courier delivery.
- ChatGPT and Claude, on the stated design, see a checked answer rather than the stored records. For the family, HIPAA does not apply even if an assistant saw the records. For a later covered-entity deployment, an assistant that receives electronic PHI to perform a function for the covered entity is likely a business associate. The October 21, 2026 HHS example of a chatbot on a provider portal is that case. An assistant that receives only a non-PHI checked answer, or that the individual uses on the individual's own behalf, is the 2016 consumer scenario and is unlikely a business associate. The fact that flips it is what the assistant actually receives and who engaged it.
- (c) Vendor only sells software to consumers. Unlikely a business associate. Selling the same software to a covered entity is still unlikely a business associate until the vendor needs access. A future consumer product, sold to households, stays in (c) and (d).
- (d) Personal-health-record vendor offered to individuals, not on behalf of a covered entity. Unlikely a business associate. If the maker later offers one version through a clinic or health plan and keeps a separate direct-to-consumer version, HIPAA follows only the covered-entity version, and only if the datasets stay separate. That is both the Omnibus preamble and OCR's 2016 health-plan scenario.

### Gaps
- NEAR AI Cloud's actual retention, logging, and key-management practices were not reviewed. The conclusion uses the product fact of a confidential-computing trusted execution environment, plus the assumption that the maker does not receive unencrypted records in the ordinary design. A design in which NEAR or the maker can decrypt, or keeps prompts, should be re-analyzed. It still would not create HIPAA status for a family-only deployment.
- No OCR guidance was found that decides whether purely ephemeral inference, with no persistence and no key, is "maintaining" PHI or is a conduit. The cited materials point toward business-associate status whenever a covered entity is the customer and the cloud processes or stores the data, and they leave a narrow, fact-specific opening only for mere transmission.

## What do HHS guidance, the 2013 Omnibus Rule, and 2024–2026 developments say, and did any of them change who is covered?

### Takeaway
The covered-entity and business-associate analysis for this product is the 2000–2013 regulatory text plus the 2016 app scenarios and cloud guidance. Those HHS pages were still up on November 8, 2026, and the business-associate FAQ was last reviewed on October 21, 2026. No 2024, 2025, or 2026 final rule was found that makes a household, or a direct-to-consumer software maker, a covered entity or business associate. Two developments are real and do not change that answer: a 2024 reproductive-health Privacy Rule amendment was largely vacated in 2025, and a January 6, 2025 Security Rule proposal was still a proposal.

### Cited Findings
- Developer-facing HHS materials still point to the 2016 scenarios. The health-apps page is the portal; the scenario analysis is the PDF cited above. ## What later facts would make HIPAA apply, and how is that different from a family self-hosting?

### Takeaway
Depends. HIPAA becomes likely when a covered entity enters the relationship: the maker contracts with a covered entity, the maker or its cloud receives electronic PHI to perform a function for a covered entity, or a clinic or a covered health plan deploys the device for patients or members and the vendor handles that information. A family operating its own device is a different fact pattern and stays unlikely. Signing a business-associate agreement does not create status if the definition is not met, and failing to sign one does not avoid status if the definition is met.

### Cited Findings
- Business-associate status attaches when the person creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a covered entity or business associate and otherwise meets the definition, whether or not a contract is signed. — Omnibus preamble, [78 Fed. Reg. 5566](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- Mere sale of software to a covered entity is not enough if the vendor has no access. Hosting or troubleshooting access is enough. — [HHS software-vendor FAQ](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/is-software-vendor-business-associate/index.html) (last reviewed December 28, 2022)
- A provider that contracts for patient-management services is a yes. A provider that only recommends an app, and a consumer-directed interoperability link, are nos. A health-plan-branded personal health record is a yes for that product only. — [OCR 2016 scenarios](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-health-app-developer-scenarios-2-2016.pdf)
- A group health plan is a health plan, and therefore a covered entity, only if it has 50 or more participants or is administered by an entity other than the employer that established and maintains it. That limit is in both the statute and the regulation. Employment records held by a covered entity in its role as employer are excluded from PHI. — [42 U.S.C. § 1320d(5)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1320d); [45 C.F.R. § 160.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-160/subpart-A/section-160.103) (group health plan definition and PHI exclusion)
- A cloud provider or subcontractor that maintains encrypted electronic PHI for a covered entity, without the key, is still a business associate. — [HHS cloud guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html) (last reviewed December 23, 2022)
- If the maker serves both a covered entity and consumers, HIPAA applies only to the PHI handled for the covered entity, and the direct-to-consumer information can stay outside if it is kept separate. — Omnibus preamble and OCR 2016 health-plan scenario, sources above

### Inferences
- Family self-hosting, including a later consumer product sold to families: unlikely HIPAA, for the maker and the family. Open-source distribution does not change that.
- The flip, likely HIPAA for the maker as a business associate, and for NEAR or Google if they maintain or process the electronic PHI: a clinic that already transmits standard transactions hires the maker, or deploys the device, so that patient information is created, received, maintained, or transmitted for the clinic. Same analysis if a covered group health plan offers the device and the maker or the cloud handles member information for the plan. Support staff who can see electronic PHI, a hosted instance, or a model-improvement pipeline that receives it, are the software-vendor FAQ's "access" examples. Encryption and the trusted execution environment do not avoid that result under the no-view guidance.
- The non-flip: a clinic or doctor recommends the device, the family buys or builds it, and any link to the clinic is started and controlled by the family. OCR's 2016 recommendation and interoperability scenarios say the developer is not a business associate. The clinic remains a covered entity for records the clinic itself still holds. Nothing in those scenarios relieves the clinic of its own HIPAA duties when the clinic transmits PHI.
- Employer deployment depends. An employer is not a covered entity just because it employs people. A covered group health plan is. A plan under 50 participants that the employer self-administers is outside the health-plan definition. Giving employees a consumer device, with the employer and the plan never receiving the health records, looks like the direct-to-consumer scenario. Offering it as a plan tool, with the vendor handling plan information, looks like OCR's health-plan personal-health-record scenario. Which one it is depends on who pays, who receives the records, and whether the plan meets the 50-participant or third-party-administrator test.
- A future contract with a covered entity covers the work done for that covered entity. It does not, under the Omnibus and the 2016 dual-product scenario, automatically convert the separate family product, if the data stay separate.

### Gaps
- "Deploy" was not given an operational definition in the product facts. Who holds the keys, who can remote in, and whether a clinic account is the same tenant as a family account would decide the close cases. Those facts were not invented here.

## What HIPAA does not require of a tool that is neither a covered entity nor a business associate, and what is the "HIPAA compliant" mistake?

### Takeaway
If the family and the maker are outside the definitions, the HIPAA Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules do not impose duties on them. There is no HIPAA requirement to do a risk analysis, sign business-associate agreements, issue a notice of privacy practices, honor HIPAA access or amendment rights, or notify HHS of a breach. Calling the product "HIPAA compliant" in that posture is a category mistake: HHS says an entity that is neither a covered entity nor a business associate does not have to comply. State law and the FTC can still apply. The FTC Health Breach Notification Rule is a different regime and was not analyzed.

### Cited Findings
- "If an entity does not meet the definition of a covered entity or business associate, it does not have to comply with the HIPAA Rules." — [HHS, Covered Entities and Business Associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/index.html)
- Privacy Rule applicability: except as otherwise provided, Subpart E applies to covered entities with respect to PHI. 45 C.F.R. § 164.500(a). The individual-rights and notice sections sit in that subpart: authorization § 164.508, notice of privacy practices § 164.520, access § 164.524, amendment § 164.526, accounting § 164.528, administrative requirements § 164.530. Business-associate contract rules are § 164.502(e) and § 164.504(e), and they govern disclosures by covered entities and business associates, not by families. — [45 C.F.R. Part 164](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164) (section headings confirmed in the eCFR text retrieved November 8, 2026)
- OCR told app developers who are outside HIPAA that privacy and security still matter, and pointed them to FTC guidance on mobile-app security and marketing, not to the HIPAA Security Rule. — [Health App Use Scenarios & HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-health-app-developer-scenarios-2-2016.pdf)
- Norton Rose Fulbright, February 27, 2026: the FTC regulates consumer-protection aspects of digital health, including data privacy, false advertising, and deceptive marketing, and it oversees non-HIPAA health data under unfair or deceptive practices. State laws such as the CCPA/CPRA can cover health data outside HIPAA. The chapter states this as a general regulatory map, not as a finding about this product. — [ICLG USA chapter, February 27, 2026](https://iclg.com/practice-areas/digital-health-laws-and-regulations/usa/)
- HITECH § 13407 is the boundary marker for the other breach rule: vendors of personal health records and certain related non-HIPAA entities notify affected individuals and the FTC. The Omnibus preamble distinguishes that FTC regime from the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule, which follows business-associate status. — [HITECH § 13407 enrolled text](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/pdf/StimulusPassedBill-SubD.pdf); [78 Fed. Reg. 5566](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)

### Inferences
- For this product, while it stays a family-operated tool and the maker does not act for a covered entity, HIPAA does not require a business-associate agreement with Google, NEAR, OpenAI, or Anthropic; a HIPAA risk analysis; a HIPAA security officer; a notice of privacy practices; or HIPAA breach notices to individuals, the media, or HHS.
- The common mistake is marketing the device or the company as "HIPAA compliant" as if that were a legal status or a government certification. HHS's rule is that non-covered, non-business-associate entities do not have to comply. No HHS product-certification program was found in the sources reviewed. A security design can be described as aligned with particular safeguards. That is not the same sentence as "HIPAA applies" or "HHS has found this compliant." The FTC's deception authority is a separate reason not to imply a legal status the product does not have. No FTC case about this product was reviewed.
- Voluntary use of strong encryption, access controls, or confidential computing is allowed. It is not a HIPAA duty in this posture, and it is not what makes HIPAA apply or not apply.

### Gaps
- This note did not research whether any specific "HIPAA compliant" seal or HHS FAQ says "we do not certify products." The negative claim is limited to: no such certification program appeared in the HHS pages reviewed.
- Which state consumer-privacy or medical-confidentiality statutes would apply to this device, and whether the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule applies to the maker, were left to other research. The only point established here is that those regimes are not the HIPAA Rules and can exist precisely because HIPAA does not.

## Do the Security Rule and the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule apply only if the entity is a covered entity or a business associate?

### Takeaway
Yes. Both rules regulate covered entities and business associates with respect to protected health information. They do not regulate a family or a software maker that is neither. They are not the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, which is aimed at personal-health-record vendors and related entities that are outside HIPAA.

### Cited Findings
- 45 C.F.R. § 164.104: Part 164 applies to health plans, clearinghouses, and providers who transmit health information electronically in connection with a covered transaction, and, where provided, to a business associate. — [45 C.F.R. Part 164](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164)
- Security Rule applicability, 45 C.F.R. § 164.302: "A covered entity or business associate must comply with the applicable standards, implementation specifications, and requirements of this subpart with respect to electronic protected health information of a covered entity." Source note: 78 Fed. Reg. 5693 (Jan. 25, 2013). The general requirements at § 164.306 are likewise addressed to covered entities and business associates: confidentiality, integrity, and availability; protection against reasonably anticipated threats; protection against reasonably anticipated impermissible uses or disclosures; workforce compliance. — same eCFR retrieval, November 8, 2026
- HHS's Security Rule summary: the rule applies to health plans, health care clearinghouses, health care providers that transmit health information electronically in connection with a standard transaction, and business associates. HITECH § 13401 is what made the Security Rule's safeguards apply directly to business associates. — [Summary of the HIPAA Security Rule, HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/security/laws-regulations/index.html) (retrieved November 8, 2026)
- Breach Notification Rule: § 164.404 requires a covered entity to notify individuals after a breach of unsecured protected health information. § 164.410 requires a business associate to notify the covered entity without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery. The breach definition in § 164.402 is an impermissible acquisition, access, use, or disclosure of protected health information under the Privacy Rule. Protected health information, by definition, is held by a covered entity or business associate. — [45 C.F.R. Part 164, §§ 164.400–164.414](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164) (text retrieved November 8, 2026)
- The other rule: HITECH § 13407 requires vendors of personal health records and listed non-HIPAA entities to notify individuals and the FTC. The Omnibus preamble says a subcontractor that is a HIPAA business associate is under the HIPAA breach rule rather than the FTC rule. — [HITECH § 13407 enrolled text](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/pdf/StimulusPassedBill-SubD.pdf); [78 Fed. Reg. 5566](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2013/01/25/2013-01073/modifications-to-the-hipaa-privacy-security-enforcement-and-breach-notification-rules-under-the)
- The January 6, 2025 Security Rule NPRM would amend duties of regulated entities. It is not final. See the previous section. It does not, in its summary, extend the Security Rule to non-covered households. — [90 Fed. Reg. 898 (Jan. 6, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/06/2024-30983/hipaa-security-rule-to-strengthen-the-cybersecurity-of-electronic-protected-health-information)

### Inferences
- Unlikely that either HIPAA rule requires anything of this product in the family-operated design. Section 164.302's phrase "of a covered entity" is the cleanest statement: the Security Rule is about a covered entity's electronic PHI. A family's files are not that.
- If the maker later becomes a business associate, both rules would likely apply to the electronic PHI handled for the covered entity, including the no-view cloud case. They still would not apply to a separate body of direct-to-consumer family data kept apart from that work.
- Do not answer a HIPAA breach question with the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, or the reverse. One applies to covered entities and business associates. The other applies to certain personal-health-record vendors and related entities that are not covered entities or business associates. Which box this maker falls into for the FTC rule was not decided here.

### Gaps
- Enforcement examples against consumer app makers for being covered entities were not found, and none were invented. OCR enforcement discussed in the Security Rule NPRM is enforcement against regulated entities.
- Whether a 2025 or 2026 amendment to 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.302, 164.306, 164.404, or 164.410 was published after the January 6, 2025 proposal was not confirmed beyond the HHS Security Rule history page and the secondary reports that no final Security Rule had issued. Last verified primary dates: eCFR text as retrieved November 8, 2026, and the January 6, 2025 proposed rule.
