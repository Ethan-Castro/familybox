# Minors, COPPA, and teen health records on a parent-administered device

Orientation as of November 8, 2026. Not legal advice. Applicability labels below are likely / unlikely / depends for (a) a parent-administered household device that is not offered as a commercial service and (b) a possible future consumer product marketed to families. Every material claim is tied to a source retrieved for this note. Where a docket was not re-checked through November 8, 2026, the status is marked unverified.

Product facts used only to frame applicability: a Raspberry Pi the parents administer; it stores family records, including a teenager’s health records, Social Security number, passport, and tax data; consumer assistants can query it, with answers passing through a confidential-computing cloud model and a check before the assistant sees them; optional Google Drive import and client-side-encrypted backup; hackathon now, possible later consumer product.

## COPPA: who it covers, the 2025 amendments, and a parent typing a teen’s data

### Takeaway

COPPA, 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501 et seq. and 16 C.F.R. Part 312, regulates operators of commercial websites and online services that are directed to children under 13, or that have actual knowledge they are collecting personal information from a child under 13. It does not regulate teenagers ages 13 through 17, and “collection” is collection from a child. A private parent-administered household device is unlikely to be a COPPA operator. A future commercial product is unlikely to be covered merely because it stores a 13-to-16-year-old’s records, and becomes a COPPA question only if it collects personal information from a user under 13. The 2025 amendments are in effect as of November 2026; they expanded personal information and added a standalone mixed-audience definition, and they did not replace the under-13 line or the actual-knowledge standard for general-audience services.

### Cited Findings

- Congress enacted COPPA in 1998. The statute is 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501 et seq. The rule requires operators of websites or online services directed to children under 13, or with actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information from children under 13, to give parents notice and obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting, using, or disclosing that information. — [Federal Register, Children’s Online Privacy Protection Rule, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)
- The 2025 final rule amendments were published April 22, 2025, as Document No. 2025-05904, RIN 3084-AB20, 90 FR 16918–16983. Effective date: June 23, 2025. Compliance date: April 22, 2026, except for 16 C.F.R. § 312.11(d)(1), (d)(4), and (g), which were not given that extra year. By November 8, 2026, both the effective date and the general compliance date have passed. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)
- The Commission’s summary of what the final amendments actually change: a new definition of “mixed audience website or online service”; online contact information expanded to mobile telephone numbers; personal information expanded to government-issued identifiers and to biometric identifiers that can be used for automated or semi-automated recognition of an individual; clarifications of “support for the internal operations” and of evidence used to decide whether a site is directed to children; changes to direct and online notices, information security, deletion, and retention; Safe Harbor assessment and reporting; and amendments on parental consent, methods of verifiable parental consent, and exceptions to parental consent. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)
- The current regulatory definition, retrieved from the eCFR for this note, still says “Child means an individual under the age of 13.” Nothing in the retrieved 2025 preamble or the current § 312.2 raises that line to 13–16 or 18. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)
- “Collects or collection means the gathering of any personal information from a child by any means,” including (1) requesting, prompting, or encouraging a child to submit personal information online, (2) enabling a child to make personal information publicly available in identifiable form, and (3) passive tracking of a child online. The definition is “including but not limited to” those three, but every listed means is collection from the child. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)
- “Operator” means a person who operates a website on the internet or an online service and collects or maintains personal information from or about users or visitors, or on whose behalf that information is collected, where the site or service is operated for commercial purposes in interstate or foreign commerce. The definition excludes a nonprofit that would be exempt from FTC Act coverage under 15 U.S.C. § 45. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)
- Personal information is “individually identifiable information about an individual collected online.” The current list expressly includes a government-issued identifier, “such as a Social Security, State identification card, birth certificate, or passport number”; a photograph, video, or audio file containing a child’s image or voice; and a biometric identifier that can be used for automated or semi-automated recognition, with examples that include voiceprints, facial templates, faceprints, and genetic data including a DNA sequence. Item (11) covers information concerning the child or the parents that the operator collects online from the child and combines with an identifier in the definition. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2); expansion beyond the prior Social Security number example described in [Gibson Dunn, FTC Updates to the COPPA Rule](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/)
- The new mixed-audience definition does not expand which sites are “directed to children.” Mixed-audience sites remain a subset of child-directed sites: directed to children under the multi-factor test, but not targeting children as the primary audience. They may not collect personal information from any visitor, other than for the limited purposes in § 312.5(c), before collecting age information or using another neutral means to determine whether the visitor is a child. They may use the existing § 312.5(c) parental-consent exceptions. A mixed-audience site is not deemed directed to children as to a visitor not identified as under 13. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)
- The preamble states the Commission is adopting amendments to parental-consent requirements and to the exceptions. A law-firm account of the final rule, consistent with that preamble, says operators must obtain separate verifiable parental consent to disclose a child’s personal information to third parties for targeted advertising or other purposes that are not “integral” to the site or service, and may not condition access on that separate consent. The same account says retention must be limited to what is reasonably necessary for the specific collection purpose, indefinite retention is prohibited, and a written retention policy is required. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/)
- The 2024 proposal to codify a school-authorization exception to verifiable parental consent was not finalized. The Commission said it was not finalizing the proposed ed-tech and school amendments “at this time” because of possible conflict with planned FERPA regulation changes, and that it would keep enforcing COPPA in schools under existing guidance. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)
- The preamble says the Commission decided not to add further biometric exceptions beyond the proposed § 312.5(c)(9) exception for collection of an audio file containing a child’s voice. This note did not separately retrieve the current text of § 312.5, so the operative sentence is the preamble’s statement of what the Commission adopted. — [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918 (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)
- A January 16, 2025 firm note, written before Federal Register publication, said a presidential regulatory freeze might require the incoming FTC chair to approve the rule before publication, and quoted then-Commissioner Ferguson’s concurrence criticizing the separate third-party consent requirement, the indefinite-retention ban, and the lack of an age-verification exception. The rule was in fact published on April 22, 2025, and the eCFR text retrieved for this note contains the amended definitions. No source retrieved for this note shows a later repeal or stay of those amendments. — [Skadden (Jan. 2025)](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-long-awaited-child-online-privacy); [Federal Register (Apr. 22, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)
- Gibson Dunn’s summary of coverage matches the statute and rule: COPPA applies to operators of commercial websites and online services directed to children under 13 that collect, use, or disclose personal information from children; operators of general-audience sites or services with actual knowledge they are collecting, using, or disclosing personal information from children under 13; and sites or services with actual knowledge they are collecting personal information directly from users of another child-directed site or service. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/)
- A violation of a COPPA regulation is an unfair or deceptive act or practice. An FTC amicus brief cites 15 U.S.C. § 6502(c) for that consequence. — [FTC amicus brief, Shanahan](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ShanahanAmicusBriefFiled.pdf)
- The FTC’s longstanding public COPPA FAQ URL returned HTTP 404 when fetched for this note (`https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions`). A site-limited search did not return a current FAQ page stating, in a quotable current document, that information collected from parents is outside COPPA. Older FTC presentation materials still describe the basic duty as notice and consent before collecting personal information from children under 13. — [FTC Student Privacy and EdTech slides](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1255643/student_privacy_and_ed_tech_all.pdf)

### Inferences

- Household device, hackathon, parents administer it and are not offering a commercial website or online service: COPPA is unlikely to apply. The rule’s “operator” definition requires a commercial website or online service. A private family installation is not that operator. A commercial cloud model in the path could be a separate operator; that is the cloud provider’s question, not the parent’s, unless the hackathon team itself operates that service for commercial purposes.
- Future consumer product marketed to families, used for a teenager who is 13 through 17: COPPA is unlikely to apply to that teenager’s records. “Child” stops at under 13. Storing a 15-year-old’s SSN, passport, or health file does not, by itself, trigger COPPA.
- Future product, and a child under 13 uses the assistant or the device and the service collects a name, voice, government ID, photo, or other personal information from that child: coverage depends. It depends on whether the seller is an “operator” of a commercial online service, whether the service is directed to children or is mixed-audience, and, if it is general-audience, whether the operator has actual knowledge the user is under 13. A parent telling the service “this profile is my 10-year-old,” or the child submitting a birth date under 13, is the fact pattern that creates actual knowledge. The 2025 rule did not, in the sources above, replace actual knowledge with “reasonably likely” or “should have known” for general-audience services.
- Parent types the child’s or teen’s data into a device the parent bought: this is unlikely to be “collection from a child.” The regulatory verb is gathering personal information from a child, and the examples are prompting the child, the child posting, and tracking the child. A parent keying in a passport number or uploading a record the parent already holds is not one of those examples. Residual uncertainty remains because the definition says “including but not limited to,” and the current FTC FAQ page could not be retrieved. See Gaps.
- Child or teen uses the assistant themselves: for a user under 13, this is the stronger COPPA fact pattern, because a prompt that asks the child to submit information, a voice interaction that captures the child’s voice, or passive tracking of that child fits § 312.2’s collection definition. For a user 13 or older, that same interaction is outside COPPA’s age line even if the assistant is the one asking the questions.
- Sending an answer onward to ChatGPT or Claude, or importing from Google Drive: if the information was collected from a child under 13, a later disclosure to a third party that is not mere support for internal operations can be a COPPA “disclosure,” and the 2025 amendments add a separate consent requirement when the disclosure is not integral to the service. If the information was typed by the parent and was never collected from a child, that consent machinery is unlikely to be the right frame. Whether a confidential-computing model is the operator’s service provider or a third party is a factual question this note does not resolve.
- The 2025 changes that matter for this product, if it ever collects from a child under 13, are the express inclusion of passport and other government-issued identifiers, voiceprints and other biometrics, the written retention limit, and separate consent for non-integral third-party disclosure. They do not pull 13-to-16-year-olds into COPPA.

### Gaps

- The current FTC COPPA FAQ set was not retrieved (the historical URL 404’d). This note therefore does not quote an FTC FAQ sentence that “collection from a parent about a child is not collection from a child.” The conclusion rests on the statute, the preamble, and § 312.2.
- The current eCFR text of 16 C.F.R. § 312.5 (the consent exceptions, including any final § 312.5(c)(9) voice exception and the separate third-party consent provision) was not fetched. Those points are taken from the April 22, 2025 preamble and from Gibson Dunn’s description of the final rule.
- No source retrieved for this note confirms there was no further FTC amendment, stay, or enforcement policy change between the eCFR text observed for this note and November 8, 2026, other than the fact that the eCFR currently displays the amended definitions.
- Whether a particular confidential-computing vendor, or OpenAI or Anthropic, is an “operator” or a “third party” when it processes a query was not researched.

## State laws that raise the age above under-13

### Takeaway

Several states regulate online services used by minors through age 17, not just under 13. For a future commercial product, the New York Child Data Protection Act is the clearest verified fit because it expressly covers a “connected device,” is in effect, and can be triggered by actual knowledge that a user is under 18. Maryland’s Age-Appropriate Design Code appears to have remained in effect through at least July 2026, with a constitutional case still open and no injunction found in the opinions retrieved. California’s Age-Appropriate Design Code is only partly enjoined after a March 12, 2026 Ninth Circuit opinion. Connecticut’s minor provisions reach under 18 and were amended again effective July 1, 2026, according to a law-firm alert whose session-law text was not separately retrieved. Florida’s HB 3 and Texas’s SCOPE Act are social-media and social-interaction statutes; they are unlikely to cover this household records device. A finalized California Privacy Protection Agency age-estimation rule implementing AB 2273 was not found.

### Inferences

- Household device that is not offered to the public as a product or service: state children’s-code and consumer-privacy statutes are unlikely to apply to the family itself. They are written for operators, controllers, and digital service providers. They can still matter to a commercial cloud or assistant in the path. That is those companies’ coverage, not a parent’s.
- Future consumer product offered to families, including in New York: NYCDPA applicability depends, and it is the state law most likely to matter. The statute’s own guidance covers a connected device. A family product is probably not “primarily directed to minors.” It can still have actual knowledge if the parent creates a profile that records a 15-year-old’s age. Processing strictly necessary to provide the parent-requested service, including data the parent supplies, can proceed without the teen’s separate consent under the Attorney General’s reading. Sending records to an assistant, training, advertising, or any use beyond that service needs the 13-to-17-year-old’s consent if the operator is covered. Under-13 users are pointed back to COPPA.
- Future product and Maryland: applicability depends. The Kids Code, as described in the sources above, reaches online products reasonably likely to be accessed by children under 18, which a family product could be. As of the November 24, 2025 opinion the law remained in effect, and the July 7, 2026 order did not enjoin it. An injunction entered after July 7, 2026 was not found and is unverified. Do not treat Maryland as enjoined.
- Future product and California AB 2273: applicability depends, and the statute is split. Data-use and dark-pattern restrictions remained preliminarily enjoined as of March 12, 2026. The injunctions against the coverage definition and the age-estimation requirement were vacated, so those parts are not blocked by that preliminary order, but the case continues and severability of the notice-and-cure provision was remanded. This note does not establish that the California Privacy Protection Agency has issued enforceable age rules under AB 2273. Status of any CPPA age rule, and of AB 1949, is not verified.
- Future product and Connecticut: applicability depends. The duties run to a controller that offers an online service, product, or feature and has actual knowledge or willfully disregards that the user is a minor under 18. A small hackathon-turned-product may or may not meet the general CTDPA controller thresholds; firm commentary says the minor provisions can reach entities that miss those thresholds, but this note did not retrieve the statutory threshold proviso. If covered, targeted advertising and sale of a 13-to-17-year-old’s data are the restricted acts. A private family archive that does not sell data or advertise is a weaker fit than a service that does. The July 1, 2026 “regardless of consent” change is included only as Wiley describes it.
- Florida HB 3 and Texas SCOPE: unlikely for this product. Both are aimed at social media or digital services that let users interact socially or that use addictive-feed features. A parent-administered records device that answers questions about family documents does not, on the facts given, create minor social-media accounts or connect users for social interaction. Do not treat the Eleventh Circuit’s October 14, 2026 reversal, or the Fifth Circuit’s July 24, 2026 partial affirmance, as a holding about household health-record devices.
- Ages 13–16 are outside COPPA and inside NYCDPA, the Connecticut minor provisions, and, if the operator is covered and the law is enforceable, the Maryland and California design codes. The design age line that matters for a commercial version is under 13 versus 13–17, not a single “minor” switch.

## Teen confidential care, parent access, and the HIPAA reproductive-health rule

### Takeaway

Laws that let a minor consent to certain care, and that limit parent access to those records, bind health care providers, insurers, and other holders of the clinical record. They do not, on the sources below, forbid a parent from reading a copy the parent already lawfully has, or from storing that copy on a device the parent bought. HIPAA’s parent-access rules likewise bind covered entities and their business associates. The design risk is different: pulling records straight from a portal, insurer, or clinic can become a way to defeat confidentiality rules those holders must follow, and marketing that parents can see “all” of a teen’s medical records can be false because those holders are often required to withhold some of them. The 2024 HIPAA reproductive-health privacy amendments were vacated nationwide on June 18, 2025, and the appeal was dismissed in September 2025; that vacatur does not remove the ordinary parent-and-minor rules in 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(g) or state minor-consent laws.

### Cited Findings

- HHS Office for Civil Rights guidance on personal representatives, citing 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(g), says a parent is usually the personal representative of an unemancipated minor and can exercise the minor’s HIPAA rights, because the parent usually has authority to make health care decisions. The same guidance lists three situations in which the parent is not the personal representative for the related information: the minor consents to care for which state or other law does not require parental consent; someone other than the parent is authorized to consent and does so; or the parent agrees to a confidential relationship between the minor and the provider. Even then, the Privacy Rule defers to state law that expressly addresses parental access: a covered entity must permit access when state law requires it and must refuse access when state law prohibits it. If state law is silent and a parent is not the personal representative, a covered entity has discretion, exercised by a licensed professional, consistent with state law. Content last reviewed January 5, 2024. — [HHS OCR, Personal Representatives](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/personal-representatives/index.html)
- An OCR letter dated December 3, 2025 tells covered entities the same three exceptions and says that, outside them, denying a parent access to a minor’s records, or demanding the minor’s authorization when no law requires it, can violate the Privacy Rule. ### Inferences

- A parent looking at a paper copy, a portal download the portal already released to that parent, or a file the parent typed into a device the parent owns is unlikely to be the person these statutes regulate. California § 123115 tells a minor’s representative they are not entitled to inspect patient records held by the provider. Washington RCW 70.02.130 allocates who may exercise patient rights under the medical-records chapter. HIPAA tells covered entities who counts as the personal representative. None of the sources above impose those duties on a parent or on a household device that is not a provider, plan, or business associate.
- The device is unlikely to be a HIPAA covered entity. The December 3, 2025 letter confines the Rules to health plans, clearinghouses, most providers, and their business associates. A future vendor could become a business associate if it provides services to a clinic or plan involving protected health information. That is a different role from selling a box to a family. This note did not retrieve a business-associate agreement analysis for a consumer device.
- The design risk that is real, and different from COPPA, is ingestion from a provider, insurer, or patient portal, plus marketing. If the product only stores what a parent already received, it sits downstream of whatever the provider was willing to release. If the product connects directly to a portal or clinic and is built or sold so parents can obtain records the provider is required to withhold, it is a practical end-run around duties that still bind the provider. This note does not describe how to do that.
- “Parents can access all of a teen’s medical records” is a claim these statutes make unsafe. In California, a provider must not give a representative records of care the minor could consent to under the listed Family Code and Health & Safety Code sections, and may withhold records when access would harm the minor or the treatment relationship. In Washington, only the minor exercises the chapter 70.02 rights for information about care the minor lawfully consented to alone. A portal that follows those rules will not show the parent everything. Saying the device can surface “all” teen medical records overclaims what the health care system is allowed to hand a parent.
- The June 18, 2025 vacatur changes provider obligations about investigations and attestations related to reproductive health care. It does not create a parent right to minor-consent records, and it does not make a household device a covered entity. State minor-consent confidentiality, including California § 123115 and Washington RCW 70.02.130, still limits providers. Reproductive privacy for teens remains mostly a state-law and provider-policy question after Purl.
- Answering questions about a teen’s records to ChatGPT or Claude does not convert the parent into a covered entity. It can still spread a record the parent holds, including a sensitive record, to another company. That is a disclosure-risk and marketing-honesty problem. It is not, on these sources, a violation of the minor-consent statutes by the parent.

## Design implications as legal-risk observations

### Takeaway

The legal exposure shifts with four facts: whether the person is under 13 or 13 through 17, who types or uploads the data, whether the product is offered to the child or only to the parent, and whether records are pulled from a provider or insurer rather than stored from a copy the parent already has. COPPA is the under-13 commercial-collection regime. State connected-device and design-code laws are the 13-to-17 commercial-product regime, led on this record by New York. Minor-consent confidentiality is a provider-side limit, and the marketing risk is claiming that parents can see every teen medical record or that the product is a complete medical archive. FTC Act section 5 is the backstop if the product is directed at children or the claims deceive parents.

### Cited Findings

- COPPA’s duties attach to commercial operators collecting personal information from a child under 13, either because the service is directed to children or because the operator has actual knowledge. A violation of the COPPA rule is an unfair or deceptive act or practice under 15 U.S.C. § 6502(c). — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2); [Federal Register, 90 FR 16918](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [FTC amicus brief citing 15 U.S.C. § 6502(c)](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ShanahanAmicusBriefFiled.pdf)
- New York’s Attorney General says a parent may agree to a product for a child and the operator may process the child’s data that the parent provides, without the child’s consent, only to the extent strictly necessary for that product, and that further processing of a 13-to-17-year-old’s personal data needs that minor’s consent. Actual knowledge follows the account when the operator has learned the age. — [N.Y. Attorney General guidance](https://ag.ny.gov/child-data-protection-act-guidance)
- California providers are told not to give a minor’s representative records of specified minor-consent services. Washington allocates chapter 70.02 rights for independently consented care to the minor alone. OCR tells covered entities to follow those state limits and, outside them, generally to treat the parent as the personal representative. — [Cal. Health & Safety Code § 123115](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=123115.); [RCW 70.02.130](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=70.02.130); [OCR letter (Dec. 3, 2025)](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-letter-hipaa-privacy-rule-and-parental-access-to-minor-childrens-medical-records.pdf)
- The 2025 COPPA amendments require separate parental consent for non-integral third-party disclosures of information collected from a child under 13, and they limit retention. Government-issued identifiers, expressly including passport numbers, and voiceprints are personal information when collected online about a child. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/); [16 C.F.R. § 312.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312/section-312.2)

### Inferences

- Age. Under 13 and 13–17 are different legal regimes. For a commercial product, under-13 collection from the child is a COPPA problem: notice, verifiable parental consent, retention limits, and care before anything is passed to an outside model or assistant. Ages 13–17 are not a COPPA problem. They are a New York Child Data Protection Act problem if the operator knows the user’s age, and they can be a Connecticut, Maryland, or California design-code problem depending on coverage and on what remains enforceable. A single “family member” profile that does not record age hides the fact that triggers actual knowledge, but prompting a child for a birth date, or marketing the product at children, creates the fact pattern these statutes use.
- Who types the data. Parent-entered records, including a teen’s SSN, passport, and tax data, are the weaker COPPA case because collection must be from a child. They are still the teen’s sensitive identifiers. If a commercial operator then uses them for a purpose beyond the service the parent requested, New York’s guidance says the 13-to-17-year-old’s consent is required. A child using the assistant, especially with voice, is the stronger COPPA case when that child is under 13, because voice and a voiceprint are personal information and the collection definitions include prompting a child and capturing a child’s voice.
- Whether the device is offered to the child. A parent-only setup, with the child not a user, stays closer to the parent-provided-data path in the New York guidance and further from COPPA’s “from a child” path. Offering the assistant or the device to the child, with a child account or child-directed prompts, is what makes a general product look child-directed or gives the operator actual knowledge. Mixed-audience treatment under the 2025 rule is available only if the service really is child-directed but not primarily so, and only if it age-screens neutrally before collecting. A family records product should not assume it can age-screen its way out of duties it never had, and should not assume a teen toggle turns off under-13 duties if younger children can use it.
- Ingestion from a provider, insurer, or portal. Storing a copy the parent already downloaded is the lower-risk side of the confidential-care line. Connecting to a clinic, insurer, or portal and presenting the result as the teen’s complete record is the higher-risk side, because California, Washington, and HIPAA require those holders to withhold some minor-consent information from parents. The product can truthfully say it stores and answers questions about records the parent has placed on it. It cannot truthfully promise that parents can see all of a teenager’s medical records.
- What not to claim. Claims to avoid, because the cited law contradicts them if stated as absolutes: “parents can access all of a teen’s medical records”; “this includes confidential reproductive, STI, mental health, and substance-use records”; “COPPA does not apply because a parent owns the device,” if children under 13 are users of a commercial service; “teen data is private from assistants,” if answers are delivered to ChatGPT, Claude, or a cloud model. FTC Act section 5, including through 15 U.S.C. § 6502(c) when COPPA applies, is the enforcement hook for a commercial operator that is directed to children or that deceives parents about what the product collects and who can see it.
- Household project versus product. For the hackathon device as described, COPPA is unlikely, the state design codes are unlikely to bind the family, HIPAA is unlikely to bind the device, and minor-consent statutes are unlikely to bind a parent who is looking at records the parent already has. For a marketed product, COPPA is unlikely for 13-to-17-year-olds and depends for under-13 users; NYCDPA is the statute most likely to apply in New York once the operator knows a user’s age; Maryland and California depend on coverage and on litigation that is not finished; Connecticut depends on controller status and actual knowledge; Florida and Texas social-media statutes are unlikely to apply. Across both versions, the confidential-care issue is a product-design and marketing issue about provider records, not a ban on a parent filing a copy at home.

### Gaps

- No enforcement action retrieved for this note applies COPPA, NYCDPA, or a state design code to a parent-administered local records device. The applicability labels are applications of the statutory texts above, not agency guidance about this product category.
- Section 5 deception cases about “complete” teen medical-record claims were not collected. The risk stated here is that the claim conflicts with provider-side law, not that a particular FTC complaint already says so.
- This note does not set out a compliance program, a consent flow, or any method for obtaining records a provider has withheld.
