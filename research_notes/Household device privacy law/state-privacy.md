# State consumer health and privacy laws for a household device

Orientation notes as of November 8, 2026. Not legal advice. Dates below are the source’s own date when one was stated, plus the retrieval date for pages read in this pass. “Not verified past [date]” means this pass did not confirm later amendments, opinions, or decisions.

Product frame used throughout: (a) a family operates the device for its own records, including a teenager’s; (b) an open-source maker has no customers; (c) a later consumer product is sold or offered to households, with revenue, employee count, and user counts unknown. The family is both data subject and operator of its own device.

## 1. Washington My Health My Data Act (RCW 19.373)

### Takeaway

The Act regulates legal entities that do business in Washington or target products or services to Washington consumers and that decide why and how consumer health data is collected, processed, shared, or sold. “Small business” is still covered; it only had a later 2024 start date. A family storing its own records is the “consumer,” not the regulated entity. A future product offered to Washington households is the scenario most likely to be in scope, and there is no revenue or employee floor that keeps a tiny seller out. The geofence rule is a ban on location-fencing health-care facilities, not the jurisdictional test.

Applicability: (a) family household use — unlikely; (b) open-source maker with no customers — unlikely, and depends if the maker itself processes Washington residents’ consumer health data; (c) future consumer product offered to Washington households — likely.

### Cited Findings

- Chapter 19.373 RCW is the Washington My Health My Data Act, session law 2023 c 191. The RCW pages retrieved still carry that 2023 session-law citation on the sections read. — [RCW 19.373](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true) (retrieved November 8, 2026); [RCW 19.373.900](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true) (short title, 2023 c 191 s 1).
- A “regulated entity” is any legal entity that (a) conducts business in Washington, or produces or provides products or services targeted to consumers in Washington, and (b) alone or jointly determines the purpose and means of collecting, processing, sharing, or selling consumer health data. Government agencies, tribal nations, and contractors processing consumer health data for a government agency are excluded. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (23), retrieved November 8, 2026).
- A “small business” is a regulated entity that meets one or both of: (a) it collects, processes, sells, or shares consumer health data of fewer than 100,000 consumers during a calendar year; or (b) it derives less than 50 percent of gross revenue from collecting, processing, selling, or sharing consumer health data, and controls, processes, sells, or shares consumer health data of fewer than 25,000 consumers. There is no employee-count test in this definition. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (28)).
- Small-business status is not an exemption from the duties. Non-small regulated entities had to comply with the main operative sections beginning March 31, 2024. Small businesses had to comply beginning June 30, 2024. The geofence section (session law section 10) applied to all persons beginning July 23, 2023. — [RCW 19.373.060](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.060) (processor timing, including the June 30, 2024 small-business date); [Washington AG FAQ, question 1](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy) (retrieved November 8, 2026; Cooley reported an FAQ update on January 16, 2024, [Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/washington-attorney-general-publishes-updated-faq-for-my-health-my-data-act/)).
- A “processor” processes consumer health data on behalf of a regulated entity or a small business, and from March 31, 2024 (June 30, 2024 for a small-business customer) may do so only under a binding contract that sets instructions and limits. A processor that goes outside the contract is treated as a regulated entity or small business for that data. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (21)); [RCW 19.373.060](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.060).
- “Consumer” means a Washington resident, or a natural person whose consumer health data is collected in Washington, acting only in an individual or household context. It does not include a person acting in an employment context. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (7)).
- “Consumer health data” means personal information linked or reasonably linkable to a consumer that identifies the consumer’s past, present, or future physical or mental health status. The list expressly includes conditions, treatment, diagnosis, medication, vital signs, gender-affirming care information, reproductive or sexual health information, biometric data, genetic data, precise location that could reasonably indicate an attempt to acquire health services or supplies, data identifying a consumer seeking health care, and information a regulated entity, small business, or their processor processes to associate a consumer with that data, including inferences. The research exclusion is narrow (IRB-governed public or peer-reviewed research). The definition is not limited to data a business collected from a consumer in a sale; it is tied to “personal information” about a “consumer,” and the duties attach to regulated entities, small businesses, and processors. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (8)).
- No separate “purely personal or household activities” exemption for the person storing their own records appears in the exemptions section read. Exemptions are mostly for HIPAA and other regulated health, GLBA, FCRA, and FERPA information, plus specified security-incident processing. — [RCW 19.373.100](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true) (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- Jurisdictional hooks are “conducts business in Washington” or “produces or provides products or services that are targeted to consumers in Washington,” plus the purpose-and-means test. The AG’s FAQ says persons and businesses that conduct business in Washington or provide services or products to Washington, and that collect, process, share, or sell consumer health data, are generally impacted; an entity that only stores data in Washington is not a regulated entity; out-of-state processors for a regulated entity or small business must comply. — [Washington AG FAQ, question 3](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy).
- “Geofence” in the definition is a virtual boundary of 2,000 feet or less around a physical location, built from GPS, cell, Wi-Fi, RFID, or other location detection. The prohibition is different from the jurisdictional test: it is unlawful for any person to implement a geofence around an entity that provides in-person health care services where the geofence is used to identify or track consumers seeking health care, collect consumer health data, or send notifications, messages, or advertisements related to consumer health data or health care services. — [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (definition (14)); [RCW 19.373.080](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.080) (2023 c 191 s 10, retrieved November 8, 2026).
- A violation is an unfair or deceptive act in trade or commerce and an unfair method of competition for purposes of the Consumer Protection Act, chapter 19.86 RCW. The AG states that any violation is a per se Consumer Protection Act violation, enforceable by the Attorney General and by private action. — [RCW 19.373.090](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.090) (2023 c 191 s 11, retrieved November 8, 2026); [Washington AG FAQ, question 2](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy).
- A Future of Privacy Forum comparison (June 2023, before the effective date) describes the private remedy by citing the Consumer Protection Act: the AG may seek injunctions, restitution, and costs (RCW 19.86.080), and a private claimant may seek an injunction and actual damages, with discretionary treble damages capped at $25,000 (RCW 19.86.090). That chart is a secondary reading of the Consumer Protection Act, not of a My Health My Data judgment. — [FPF NV/WA chart (June 2023)](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/NV_WA-Comparison-Chart-FINAL.pdf).
- This pass found no 2025 or 2026 amendment in the RCW text retrieved. Section notes remain 2023 c 191. Not verified past the RCW pages retrieved November 8, 2026. The AG FAQ text retrieved still describes the 2023 and 2024 dates. Cooley’s report of the FAQ’s separate-homepage-link update is dated January 16, 2024. Not verified that the FAQ was revised after that Cooley report. — [RCW chapter](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true); [Cooley, January 16, 2024](https://cdp.cooley.com/washington-attorney-general-publishes-updated-faq-for-my-health-my-data-act/).
- Reported litigation that could be tied to a docket: *Maxwell v. Amazon.com, Inc.*, No. 2:25-cv-00261 (W.D. Wash.), filed February 10, 2025, and on April 14, 2025 consolidated into *In re Amazon Ads SDK Litigation*, No. 2:25-cv-00252. CourtListener’s docket summary, last updated December 23, 2025, shows the Maxwell caption terminated April 14, 2025, upon consolidation, and does not show a merits opinion. Paul Hastings (early 2025) and Proskauer describe the complaint as alleging collection of biometric and precise-location data through an advertising SDK. A secondary October 2026 compliance article says the plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed without prejudice in May and June 2025 and that the court closed the case on June 6, 2025, with no merits ruling. That dismissal sequence was not confirmed from the court docket in this pass. No published merits decision interpreting the Act was found. — [CourtListener docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69628048/maxwell-v-amazoncom-inc/) (last updated December 23, 2025); [Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/thought-leadership/biometrics-litigation-update-first-class-action-complaint-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act-is-your-company-ready); [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/my-health-my-dollar-amazons-health-data-troubles-in-washington); [Verticomply, describing status as of October 2026](https://verticomply.com/blog/washington-my-health-my-data-act-apps).

### Inferences

- Duties run against a legal entity that decides the purposes and means, not against a household because it stores health records about itself. The statute’s “household context” language defines who counts as a consumer, which is the opposite of treating the family as the regulated entity.
- “Small business” widens, rather than narrows, the set of entities that had to comply by June 30, 2024. A later product with unknown revenue and few Washington users would still be a regulated entity if it targets Washington consumers and determines why their consumer health data is processed. Employee count does not create an off-ramp.
- An out-of-state open-source project is not pulled in merely because the code could be downloaded in Washington or because a server sits in Washington. The AG’s “only stores data in Washington” answer points the other way. It is pulled in if it provides a product or service to Washington consumers and itself determines the purposes of collecting or processing their consumer health data, or if it processes that data as a processor for someone who is a regulated entity. A family running the software locally is not that someone.
- If the maker operates the confidential-computing path and the health data is processed there for Washington users of a offered product, processor-versus-regulated-entity status depends on who decides purposes and means and whether a contract limits the processor. That is a depends, not a likely, on the facts given.
- The facility geofence ban does not apply to this device on the facts given, because nothing in the product description implements a geofence around an in-person health-care entity. It also does not expand who is a regulated entity.
- The private right of action is derivative of the Consumer Protection Act. It is real as a matter of the AG’s reading and RCW 19.373.090, and it is not limited to the geofence section. What injury a plaintiff must prove, and whether treble damages are available in a particular case, has no published My Health My Data merits decision in the sources verified here.

### Gaps

- No Washington Supreme Court or AG formal opinion interpreting “targeted to consumers,” “conducts business,” or “collected in Washington” for an out-of-state open-source project was found. Not verified past November 8, 2026.
- The May–June 2025 voluntary-dismissal account of the Amazon litigation is from a secondary article. The court docket summary confirms filing and consolidation only.
- RCW 19.86.090’s private-remedy details (actual damages, fee-shifting, treble cap) were taken from a June 2023 secondary chart, not re-read from the Consumer Protection Act page in this pass.
- No 2025–2026 amendment, emergency rule, or new AG FAQ revision was found. Absence from this pass is not proof none exists.

## 2. Nevada consumer health data law (NRS 603A.400 to 603A.550; 2023 SB 370)

### Takeaway

Nevada’s consumer-health statute is narrower than Washington’s at the definition of “consumer,” has no small-business delay, bans sale without a one-year written authorization, and expressly says it creates no private right of action. It took effect March 31, 2024. A family using its own device is unlikely to be a regulated entity. A future product offered to Nevada consumers is likely in scope if the seller decides the purposes and means. The open-source maker with no customers is unlikely to be in scope unless it itself processes Nevadan consumer health data.

Applicability: (a) unlikely; (b) unlikely, depends if the maker processes Nevadan consumer health data; (c) likely.

### Cited Findings

- SB 370 was signed June 16, 2023, became Chapter 525, and is effective March 31, 2024. It is codified at NRS 603A.400 through 603A.550. — [LegiScan SB 370 status](https://legiscan.com/NV/bill/SB370/2023) (retrieved November 8, 2026); [NRS Chapter 603A](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html) (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- “Regulated entity” means any person who conducts business in Nevada or produces or provides products or services targeted to consumers in Nevada, and who alone or with others determines the purpose and means of processing, sharing, or selling consumer health data. — [NRS 603A.465](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- “Consumer” means a natural person who has requested a product or service from a regulated entity and who resides in Nevada or whose consumer health data is collected in Nevada. Employment and government-agent contexts are excluded. — [NRS 603A.425](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- “Consumer health data” means personally identifiable information linked or reasonably capable of being linked to a consumer that a regulated entity uses to identify the past, present, or future health status of the consumer. It includes conditions, interventions, procedures, medication, vital signs, reproductive or sexual health care, gender-affirming care, related biometric or genetic data, precise geolocation used to indicate an attempt to receive health care, and inferences. It excludes video-game access data and shopping-habit data not used to identify a specific health status. — [NRS 603A.430](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- There is no small-business threshold or delayed effective date in the codified health-data sections read. A June 2023 comparison noted the absence of a small-business carve-out; the official NRS text retrieved does not add one. — [NRS 603A.400–.550](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html); [FPF chart, June 2023](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/NV_WA-Comparison-Chart-FINAL.pdf).
- Collection requires affirmative voluntary consent, or must be necessary to provide a product or service the consumer requested. Sharing has the same structure, plus sharing as otherwise authorized by law. Consent requests have specified disclosures, including how to withdraw consent. — [NRS 603A.500](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- Sale restriction: a person shall not sell or offer to sell consumer health data without the consumer’s written authorization, or outside that authorization. Goods or services may not be conditioned on the authorization. The authorization must name seller and purchaser, describe the data and purpose, state the no-conditioning rule and redisclosure warning, be revocable, expire one year after it is given, and be signed. Seller and purchaser must keep a copy for at least six years after expiration. “Sell” is exchange for money or other valuable consideration, with exclusions for a processor acting consistently with the disclosed purpose, an asset sale, providing a requested product or service, an affiliate, a consumer-directed disclosure, and data the consumer intentionally made public. — [NRS 603A.475](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html); [NRS 603A.535](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- A person shall not implement a geofence within 1,750 feet of a medical facility, facility for the dependent, or other person or entity providing in-person health care services or products, for identifying or tracking consumers seeking in-person care, collecting consumer health data, or sending related notifications or ads. — [NRS 603A.540](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- A processor may process consumer health data only under a contract with a regulated entity. Processing outside the contract does not, by itself, make the processor guilty of a deceptive trade practice for that contract breach, but the processor is deemed a regulated entity for those actions. — [NRS 603A.530](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html); [NRS 603A.460](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- Except as provided for that processor provision, a violation is a deceptive trade practice under NRS 598.0903 to 598.0999. The health-data provisions do not create a private right of action and must not be construed to affect any other law. — [NRS 603A.550](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html). Bass, Berry & Sims (March 20, 2024) summarizes enforcement as primarily by the Commissioner of Consumer Affairs and/or the Attorney General, and contrasts that with Washington’s private action. — [Bass, Berry & Sims, March 20, 2024](https://bassberry.com/news/nevada-consumer-health-data-law-takes-effect-on-march-31-2024/).
- Nevada’s separate website-operator law, NRS 603A.300 to 603A.360, is a sale opt-out regime. It does not establish a private right of action against an operator. The chapter text excludes consumer health data that is subject to NRS 603A.400 to 603A.550 from the covered-information regime. — [NRS 603A.360(4)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html); exclusion listed in the same chapter (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- Not verified past the NRS page retrieved November 8, 2026. No 2025 or 2026 amendment to NRS 603A.400–.550 was found in this pass. The “Added to NRS by 2023” notes on the sections read are consistent with the 2023 enactment and do not show a later amendment.

### Inferences

- Because a “consumer” must have requested a product or service from a regulated entity, health records a family stores for itself, without requesting that storage from a Nevada business, are outside the definition even if the records identify health status.
- The sale ban and the geofence ban are written onto “a person,” so they are not limited to entities that meet the regulated-entity test. On these facts neither is triggered: the product is not described as selling health data, and it is not described as geofencing clinics. A future product that sold or licensed consumer health data for consideration would face the authorization statute even if some other party in the chain was not the regulated entity.
- There is no revenue, consumer-count, or employee threshold to grow into. The first Nevada household that is offered the product and whose health data the seller uses to identify health status is enough, if the seller conducts business in Nevada or targets products to Nevada consumers and determines purposes and means.
- Publishing open-source code, without requesting-and-providing a product to Nevada consumers and without processing their health data, is unlikely to meet “requested a product or service from a regulated entity.” Operating a cloud model that receives those users’ health data for a product offered into Nevada is the depends case, and it can be either processor or regulated entity under NRS 603A.530.
- Nevada’s general website privacy law does not add a private right of action and, where the data is consumer health data, points back to the SB 370 chapter. It is not a second health-data regime. Whether a future marketing site that collects non-health “covered information” (for example an account email, or an SSN if one were collected through the site) is an “operator” was not measured against the operator definition in this pass.

### Gaps

- The operator thresholds in Nevada’s pre-2023 website privacy law (revenue or volume exclusions inside the definition of “operator”) were not extracted. Do not treat this note as confirming or denying that a small site is an “operator.”
- No Nevada AG opinion or reported case applying NRS 603A.400–.550 to a household device or open-source project was found. Not verified past November 8, 2026.
- Civil penalty amounts under the deceptive-trade-practice chapter were not re-read from NRS 598 in this pass. The FPF chart’s fine description is June 2023 secondary material.

## 3. Connecticut Data Privacy Act, including consumer health data

### Takeaway

Connecticut has two overlapping regimes. Since October 1, 2023, consumer-health-data duties in C.G.S. § 42-526, and the Act’s provisions “concerning consumer health data and consumer health data controllers,” apply without the ordinary consumer-count or revenue threshold to a person who conducts business in Connecticut or produces products or services targeted to Connecticut residents. Since July 1, 2026, the general Act also applies, with no volume floor, to a person with that same geographic nexus who controls or processes consumers’ sensitive data. Sensitive data includes consumer health data and, as of that date, government ID numbers such as Social Security and passport numbers. Processing sensitive data requires consent. The Attorney General has exclusive enforcement. Purely personal or household processing is excluded, and Public Act 25-113 did not repeal that subsection.

Applicability: (a) family household use — unlikely; (b) open-source maker with no Connecticut users whose data it controls — unlikely; (c) a future product offered to Connecticut households — likely once it processes even one Connecticut consumer’s health data, SSN, or passport number, if a court or enforcer treats the offer as conducting business in Connecticut or targeting products to Connecticut residents. “Targeted” is not defined in the sections read.

### Cited Findings

- Public Act 22-15 created the Act, effective July 1, 2023. Public Act 23-56 added consumer health data, consumer health data controller, gender-affirming and reproductive health definitions, and § 42-526. Public Act 23-204 moved the effective date of those Public Act 23-56 provisions from July 1, 2023, to October 1, 2023. — History notes in the [2025 compilation of Chapter 743jj](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm) (the compilation itself warns readers to use the 2026 supplement, revised to January 1, 2026, for 2025-session amendments).
- Until the July 1, 2026 amendment, “consumer health data” meant any personal data that a controller uses to identify a consumer’s physical or mental health condition or diagnosis, including gender-affirming health data and reproductive or sexual health data. A “consumer health data controller” determines the purpose and means of processing consumer health data. — [C.G.S. § 42-515(9)–(10)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm), with the July 1, 2026 substitute printed at [Justia § 42-515](https://law.justia.com/codes/connecticut/title-42/chapter-743jj/section-42-515/).
- Public Act 25-113 § 5, effective July 1, 2026, inserts “or status,” so consumer health data means personal data a controller uses to identify a consumer’s physical or mental health condition, diagnosis, or status, still including gender-affirming and reproductive or sexual health data. Public Act 26-64 § 12, effective October 1, 2026, repeals and restates § 42-515 and keeps that “condition, diagnosis or status” definition. — [Public Act 25-113 PDF](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf) (signed text retrieved November 8, 2026); [Public Act 26-64 PDF](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf) (retrieved November 8, 2026). Snell & Wilmer’s 2026 amendments note, retrieved the same day, dates SB 1295 / Public Act 25-113 as enacted in 2025 and effective July 1, 2026, and SB 4 / Public Act 26-64 as effective October 1, 2026. — [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/).
- “Sensitive data” in the pre-July 2026 compilation already included data revealing a mental or physical health condition or diagnosis, consumer health data, genetic or biometric data processed to uniquely identify someone, personal data collected from a known child, crime-victim status, and precise geolocation. — [C.G.S. § 42-515](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm) (definition (38) in that compilation).
- Effective July 1, 2026, sensitive data also includes, among other additions, disability or treatment, status as nonbinary or transgender, neural data, specified financial-account credentials, and a government-issued identification number, including a Social Security number, passport number, state identification card number, or driver’s license number, that applicable law does not require to be publicly displayed. Genetic or biometric data is no longer limited to identification use. Public Act 26-64 § 12, effective October 1, 2026, carries that expanded list forward. — [Public Act 25-113 § 5](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf); [Public Act 26-64 § 12](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf).
- Ordinary applicability before July 1, 2026: a person that conducts business in Connecticut or produces products or services targeted to residents, and that in the preceding calendar year controlled or processed personal data of at least 100,000 consumers (excluding payment-only processing), or of at least 25,000 consumers and derived more than 25 percent of gross revenue from selling personal data. — [C.G.S. § 42-516](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm).
- Effective July 1, 2026, § 42-516 applies to a person that conducts business in Connecticut or produces products or services targeted to residents and that, during the preceding calendar year, (1) controlled or processed personal data of at least 35,000 consumers, excluding payment-only processing, or (2) controls or processes consumers’ sensitive data, excluding payment-only processing, or (3) offers consumers’ personal data for sale in trade or commerce. The old “25,000 consumers and 25 percent of revenue” prong is deleted. Prongs (2) and (3) have no consumer-count or revenue floor. — [Public Act 25-113 § 6](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf). Epstein Becker’s June 24, 2025 enactment write-up matches that reading and notes the employee and business-to-business exclusion continues. — [Epstein Becker](https://www.ebglaw.com/workforce-bulletin/connecticut-overhauls-its-data-privacy-act).
- Separate from those thresholds, § 42-526(a)(2) says that, notwithstanding § 42-516, subsection (a) and the provisions of §§ 42-515 and 42-517 to 42-525 concerning consumer health data and consumer health data controllers apply to persons that conduct business in Connecticut and persons that produce products or services targeted to residents. No consumer count and no revenue percentage. — [C.G.S. § 42-526(a)(2)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm).
- Consumer-health-data duties in § 42-526(a), subject to listed exemptions: do not give an employee or contractor access unless they have a contractual or statutory duty of confidentiality; do not give a processor access unless both sides comply with § 42-521; do not use a geofence within 1,750 feet of a mental health facility or reproductive or sexual health facility to identify, track, collect data from, or notify a consumer regarding the consumer’s consumer health data; do not sell or offer to sell consumer health data without consent. — [C.G.S. § 42-526(a)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm). Nonprofit organizations are exempt from the general Act under § 42-517, and the § 42-526(b) exemption list read does not repeat the nonprofit exemption for subsection (a). That difference was not briefed by an AG opinion in this pass.
- General controller duty: do not process sensitive data concerning a consumer without consent (and, for a known child, comply with COPPA). Effective July 1, 2026, and restated effective October 1, 2026, the controller also may not process sensitive data unless the processing is reasonably necessary for the purposes for which it is processed, and may not sell sensitive data without consent. Consent for a child the controller knows, or willfully disregards, is a child must follow COPPA. — [C.G.S. § 42-520(a)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm); [Public Act 25-113 § 9](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf); [Public Act 26-64 § 14](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf).
- “Consumer” means a Connecticut resident and does not include an individual acting in a commercial or employment context. — [C.G.S. § 42-515(8)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm). Public Act 25-113 amends the consumer definition effective July 1, 2026; the employment/commercial exclusion was not quoted as deleted in the portions read. Not every word of the amended consumer definition was re-copied here.
- Personal or household activities: nothing in §§ 42-515 to 42-526 shall be construed to apply to any person’s processing of personal data in the course of purely personal or household activities. — [C.G.S. § 42-524(e)(2)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm). Public Act 25-113 § 12, effective July 1, 2026, substitutes only subsections (a) through (d) of § 42-524. Public Act 26-64 § 16, effective October 1, 2026, again substitutes subsection (a). Neither public-act section, as retrieved, repeals subsection (e). — [Public Act 25-113 § 12](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf); [Public Act 26-64 § 16](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf).
- Enforcement of §§ 42-515 to 42-524 and § 42-526 is exclusively by the Attorney General. Nothing in those sections provides the basis for a private right of action. A violation is an unfair trade practice under § 42-110b, enforced solely by the Attorney General, and the private CUTPA remedy in § 42-110g does not apply. The mandatory cure period ended December 31, 2024. Beginning January 1, 2025, cure is discretionary and the Attorney General may consider the number of violations, the size and complexity of the entity, the sensitivity of the data, and other listed factors. — [C.G.S. § 42-525](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm).
- Minor protections, separate from the health-data section: a “minor” is a consumer younger than 18. Controllers that offer an online service, product, or feature to consumers they know, or willfully disregard, are minors have duties under §§ 42-529 and following, including limits on targeted advertising and sale and a reasonable-care duty. Public Act 25-113 amends those provisions effective July 1, 2026. Social-media-only unpublish/delete rights in § 42-528 are a poor fit for this device; the broader online-service minor duties are the relevant ones if a controller knowingly offers the product to a teenager. — [C.G.S. §§ 42-528, 42-529](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm); [Public Act 25-113 §§ 13–15](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf).
- Public Act 26-64 also adds, effective October 1, 2026, a data-broker registration regime (sale or license of brokered personal data in Connecticut restricted from January 1, 2027, unless registered) and a direct-to-consumer genetic-testing property right. Brokered personal data, as defined in that act, can include a Social Security number or government ID. Those provisions matter if a future product sells or licenses personal data, not if a household stores its own records. — [Public Act 26-64 §§ 1–2, 11–19](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2026/act/pa/pdf/2026PA-00064-R00SB-00004-PA.pdf).

### Inferences

- The family’s own operation is the personal-or-household exclusion in § 42-524(e)(2). Storing a teenager’s records as part of that household processing stays inside the exclusion. The exclusion does not travel with the maker once the maker processes Connecticut consumers’ data as a product.
- Before July 1, 2026, a tiny product could still be a consumer health data controller under § 42-526(a)(2) with no user-count threshold, but the general Act’s 100,000 / 25-percent tests would otherwise keep it out. After July 1, 2026, processing any Connecticut consumer’s sensitive data — health data, an SSN, or a passport number — is itself an applicability trigger, again with no count and no revenue test, if the geographic nexus is met.
- “Produces products or services that are targeted to residents of this state” is the extraterritorial hook. The statute, as read, does not say that a generally available download, with no Connecticut marketing, is “targeted.” It also does not require a Connecticut office. Treat a product offered to U.S. households, including Connecticut residents who can sign up, as depends on how “targeted” and “conducts business” are applied. No case deciding that phrase for this kind of device was found.
- Health-data controller duties are not just the general sensitive-data consent rule. They add employee/contractor confidentiality, the processor-contract cross-reference, a facility geofence ban, and a consent requirement to sell consumer health data, and they apply below the ordinary thresholds.
- There is no employee-count threshold and, after July 1, 2026, no revenue threshold that saves a small seller who processes sensitive data of Connecticut consumers. The old 25-percent-of-revenue prong is gone.
- A teenager’s data matters in two ways if the maker is in scope: consumer health data and sensitive data regardless of age, and the under-18 online-service duties if the controller knows or willfully disregards that users are minors. Those minor duties are Attorney General enforcement, not a private action, on the sections read.

### Gaps

- Whether § 42-526(a)’s no-threshold health rules apply to a nonprofit open-source maintainer is not settled by an AG opinion in this pass. The general Act exempts nonprofits in § 42-517; § 42-526(b)’s exemption list, as compiled, does not repeat “nonprofit.”
- The full amended definition of “consumer” in Public Act 25-113 was not line-checked for a change to the household or employment exclusions beyond what is noted above.
- No Connecticut AG enforcement action or reported case applying § 42-526 or the July 1, 2026 sensitive-data trigger to a household device was found. Not verified past November 8, 2026.
- Public Act 26-64’s data-broker and genetic-testing sections were not fully parsed for exemptions that might cover an encrypted backup to Google Drive.

## 4. California, New York SHIELD, Maryland, and other comprehensive laws that change the answer

### Takeaway

California’s consumer privacy law reaches a for-profit business that does business in California and meets a revenue, volume, or sell/share threshold. The revenue figure adjusted effective January 1, 2025, is $26,625,000, and that is still the figure posted by the California Privacy Protection Agency; the next odd-year adjustment would be 2027. Sensitive personal information includes health information, Social Security numbers, and passport numbers, but a family and a pre-revenue open-source maker are unlikely to be a “business.” New York’s SHIELD Act is different: the safeguard duty runs to any person or business that owns or licenses computerized private information of a New York resident, private information includes an SSN and medical information, and there is no private right of action. Maryland’s Online Data Privacy Act, effective October 1, 2025, has real consumer-health and sensitive-data limits, but only after 35,000 Maryland consumers or a 10,000-consumer-plus-sale-revenue test. Most other comprehensive state laws use similar count or sale-revenue thresholds, so they do not change the answer until a product has real user counts. Connecticut, Washington, and Nevada are the outliers that can apply with no revenue and, in Connecticut’s and Washington’s health regimes, with no large user count. This is not a 50-state survey.

Applicability, short form: California CCPA — (a) unlikely, (b) unlikely, (c) depends on the thresholds. New York SHIELD safeguards — (a) unlikely as a household, with an edge-case reading of “any person,” (b) unlikely if the maker does not own or license New Yorkers’ private information, (c) likely if the business owns or licenses computerized SSNs or medical information of New York residents. Maryland — (a) unlikely, (b) unlikely, (c) unlikely until the 35,000 or 10,000-plus-20-percent thresholds are met.

### Cited Findings

- A CCPA “business” is a for-profit (or financially beneficial) legal entity that collects consumers’ personal information, or on whose behalf it is collected, determines the purposes and means, does business in California, and meets one or more of: (A) as of January 1, annual gross revenues over $25,000,000 in the preceding calendar year, as adjusted; (B) alone or in combination, annually buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households; or (C) derives 50 percent or more of annual revenues from selling or sharing consumers’ personal information. Control/common-branding and certain joint ventures are also businesses. A non-covered person may volunteer by certifying to the Agency. — [Civ. Code § 1798.140(d)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/), FindLaw header “Current as of January 01, 2026” (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- The Agency’s CPI page, effective January 1, 2025, and posted December 17, 2024, sets § 1798.140(d)(1)(A) at $26,625,000. Adjustments occur every odd-numbered year under § 1798.199.95(d). No 2026 CPI revision was posted on that page. The statute’s printed “twenty-five million” is the base; the adjustment sits on top of it. — [CPPA monetary thresholds](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html) (retrieved November 8, 2026). The Attorney General’s FAQ still states the unadjusted “over $25 million” and the 100,000 / 50 percent tests. — [California AG CCPA FAQ](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- “Consumer” is a California resident as defined in 18 C.C.R. § 17014 as it read on September 1, 2017. “Personal information” is information that identifies or is reasonably linkable to a particular consumer or household. “Household” is consumers who cohabitate at the same residential address and share common devices or services. — [§ 1798.140](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/) (definitions of consumer, personal information, and household; household definition confirmed in the FindLaw snippet retrieved November 8, 2026).
- “Sensitive personal information” includes personal information that reveals a Social Security number, driver’s license, state identification card, or passport number; specified account credentials; precise geolocation; racial or ethnic origin, citizenship or immigration status, religious or philosophical beliefs, or union membership; the contents of mail, email, and text messages unless the business is the intended recipient; genetic data; and neural data. It also includes biometric information processed to uniquely identify a consumer, personal information collected and analyzed concerning a consumer’s health, and personal information collected and analyzed concerning a consumer’s sex life or sexual orientation. — [§ 1798.140(ae)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/).
- A “service provider” processes personal information on behalf of a business for a business purpose under a written contract that prohibits selling or sharing, retaining or using the information outside the direct business relationship, and specified combining. Service-provider status is derivative of a business. It is not a separate way for a household or a maker with no business customer to enter the Act. — [§ 1798.140(ag)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/).
- The 2025 codification of § 1798.145 retrieved from Justia does not contain a freestanding sentence exempting “purely personal or household activities” in subdivisions (a) through (r). What it does say is that the obligations in §§ 1798.105, 1798.106, 1798.110, and 1798.115 do not apply to household data. Employee and business-to-business subdivisions (m) and (n) are marked inoperative January 1, 2023. The amendment note on that page stops at Stats. 2023, ch. 567 (AB 1194), effective January 1, 2024. — [§ 1798.145](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-3/part-4/title-1-81-5/section-1798-145/) (labeled 2025 California Code, retrieved November 8, 2026).
- Cybersecurity-audit and automated-decisionmaking regulations are in effect January 1, 2026, in the Agency’s compiled regulations PDF of that date. Jackson Lewis, discussing those regulations, states that a cybersecurity audit is required only if the business already processes personal information in a way that presents a significant risk and either meets the 50-percent sell/share threshold or meets the gross-revenue threshold and processed personal information of at least 250,000 consumers or households or sensitive personal information of at least 50,000 consumers in the preceding year. The regulations’ ADMT definition, as extracted, excludes web hosting, networking, data storage, firewalls, databases, and spreadsheets when they do not replace human decisionmaking, and it excludes advertising from “significant decision.” — [CPPA regulations PDF, effective January 1, 2026](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_statute_eff_20260101.pdf); [Jackson Lewis](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/navigating-california-consumer-privacy-act-30-essential-faqs-covered-businesses-including-clarifying-regulations-effective-1126) (retrieved November 8, 2026). The audit counts are from the law-firm FAQ, not re-quoted from the regulation section number in this pass.
- New York General Business Law § 899-aa defines “private information” to include personal information in combination with a Social Security number, driver’s license or non-driver ID, specified financial-account data, biometric information, medical information (medical history, mental or physical condition, or medical treatment or diagnosis by a health care professional), or health-insurance information, when the data element or the combination is not encrypted or the key is also acquired, and separately a username or email plus a password or security question that permits account access. — [GBL § 899-aa](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-AA) (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- Section 899-bb requires any person or business that owns or licenses computerized data which includes private information of a New York resident to implement reasonable safeguards, including disposal. The retrieved text does not limit that sentence to persons who “conduct business in New York.” A small business — fewer than 50 employees, or less than $3 million gross annual revenue in each of the last three fiscal years, or less than $5 million in year-end total assets — complies with the listed safeguard program if its administrative, technical, and physical safeguards are appropriate to its size, complexity, activities, and the sensitivity of the information. Failure is enforceable by the Attorney General. “Nothing in this section shall create a private right of action.” FindLaw marks § 899-bb current as of January 1, 2026. — [GBL § 899-bb](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-BB); [FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/ny/general-business-law/gbs-sect-899-bb/); [NY AG SHIELD page](https://ag.ny.gov/resources/organizations/data-breach-reporting/shield-act) (retrieved November 8, 2026).
- Maryland’s Attorney General: the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act took effect October 1, 2025. It applies to persons who conduct business in Maryland or produce products or services targeted to residents and who in the prior calendar year controlled or processed personal data of at least 35,000 Maryland consumers, or of at least 10,000 Maryland consumers and derived more than 20 percent of gross revenue from the sale of personal data. It protects consumers acting in an individual or household context and does not protect an individual acting in an employment context. Sensitive data includes consumer health data. A controller may not sell sensitive data. Processors that handle data for covered businesses are in scope. — [Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/resources-info/Pages/data-privacy.aspx) (retrieved November 8, 2026). The official code section (generally cited as Md. Code, Com. Law § 14-4601 and following) was not opened in this pass; the AG page is the primary source used for thresholds and effective date.
- IAPP’s 2026 comprehensive-law chart, retrieved November 8, 2026, lists signed comprehensive consumer privacy laws including Alabama (effective May 1, 2027), California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware (January 1, 2025), Indiana (January 1, 2026), Iowa (January 1, 2025), Kentucky (January 1, 2026), Louisiana (January 1, 2027), Maryland (October 1, 2025), Minnesota (July 31, 2025), Montana (October 1, 2024), Nebraska (January 1, 2025), New Hampshire (January 1, 2025), New Jersey (January 15, 2025), Oklahoma (January 1, 2027), Oregon (July 1, 2024), Rhode Island (January 1, 2026), Tennessee (July 1, 2025), Texas (July 1, 2024), Utah (December 31, 2023), Vermont (January 1, 2028), and Virginia (January 1, 2023). It is a tracker, not a statute. — [IAPP chart PDF](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt90070182553bdfe6/6a429c09099ffcd63cefaad7/State_Comp_Privacy_Law_Chart.pdf); [IAPP tracker page](https://iapp.org/resources/article/us-state-privacy-legislation-tracker); [MultiState, October 28, 2026](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2026/10/28/twenty-four-states-now-have-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-laws).
- The usual pattern outside Connecticut’s 2026 sensitive-data trigger, Washington’s health act, and Nevada’s health act is a controller threshold on the order of 100,000 consumers, or a smaller count plus a share of revenue from selling personal data, plus opt-in consent for sensitive data that includes health information. Those laws change the answer for this product only after the counts are real. Indiana, Kentucky, and Rhode Island are the January 1, 2026 additions on the IAPP chart. ### Inferences

- For California, unknown revenue matters only if gross revenue exceeds $26,625,000, or the project buys, sells, or shares personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers or households, or at least half of revenue comes from selling or sharing. A hackathon project and a small early product are unlikely to be a business. Employee count is not a CCPA threshold. The January 1, 2026 cybersecurity-audit and ADMT rules sit on top of business status plus further volume or sell/share tests, so they do not independently reach a sub-threshold maker. A household question-answering device is also a poor fit for “significant decision” ADMT on the regulatory definition retrieved.
- The CCPA personal-information definition reaches household data, and certain access, deletion, and correction duties do not apply to household data. That is not the same as an exemption for a natural person processing data in the home. The for-profit business definition does that work for the family and for a noncommercial maintainer.
- New York’s safeguard law is the SSN statute that can reach an out-of-state product without a consumer-count threshold, because § 899-bb is keyed to owning or licensing a New York resident’s private information. A future product whose operator owns or licenses users’ SSNs or medical information is the likely case, including a small business, which gets a scaled security program rather than an exemption. If the household owns the data on its own device and the maker never receives the SSN or medical information, the maker is unlikely to “own or license” it. Client-side encryption and who holds the key affect the breach definition more than they erase the safeguard duty for unencrypted private information the business does hold.
- A literal reading of “any person” in § 899-bb could be argued to cover a New York resident’s family member who keeps computerized SSNs. The small-business examples and the AG materials are written for organizations. Treat household self-storage as unlikely to be the enforcement target, and flag the statutory wording as unresolved.
- Maryland’s health-data and “no sale of sensitive data” rules would matter to a consumer product of this type, and they are stricter than a consent-only sensitive-data rule, but they are gated by 35,000 consumers or the 10,000-plus-20-percent sale test. They do not change the answer at hackathon scale or at a small early launch.
- The list of comprehensive states above is the usual set as of the IAPP chart retrieved November 8, 2026. It is not exhaustive of health-privacy, biometric, or insurance statutes, and several 2026 enactments on that chart are not yet effective (Alabama, Louisiana, Oklahoma, Vermont).

## 5. State breach-notification laws

### Takeaway

Every state has a breach-notification statute. The general pattern is that a Social Security number, and medical or health-insurance information, count as the kind of personal or private information that triggers notice when acquired without authorization, usually in combination with a name and subject to encryption caveats. California Civil Code § 1798.82 and New York General Business Law § 899-aa are the two citations. They are duties that arise when a breach happens, not ongoing privacy-policy regimes. A household storing its own data is unlikely to be the intended “business,” but California’s statute is written to cover an individual as well as a business, if that individual conducts business in California.

Applicability: (a) family — unlikely for ordinary household storage; (b) open-source maker that does not own or license residents’ covered data — unlikely; (c) a future product that conducts business in a state and owns or licenses residents’ unencrypted SSNs or medical information — likely, in that state, if a breach occurs. Which of the 50 statutes applies depends on the resident’s state and on where the business conducts business. This note does not catalog them.

### Cited Findings

- California: an individual or business that conducts business in California and owns or licenses computerized data that includes personal information must disclose a breach of the security of the system to a California resident whose unencrypted personal information was, or is reasonably believed to have been, acquired by an unauthorized person, and in specified cases where encrypted information and the key or credential were acquired. The 2025 codification requires disclosure within 30 calendar days of discovery or notification, subject to law-enforcement delay and time needed to determine scope and restore integrity. A person who maintains but does not own the data must notify the owner or licensee. Notice to more than 500 California residents requires a sample copy to the Attorney General. — [Civ. Code § 1798.82](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-3/part-4/title-1-81/section-1798-82/) (labeled 2025 California Code, retrieved November 8, 2026); [California AG breach reporting](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/databreach/reporting) (retrieved November 8, 2026). Troutman, October 2025, describes a 2025 amendment to the timing rules; the 30-day sentence is in the Justia text retrieved. — [Troutman, October 2025](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/10/california-amends-breach-notification-statute/).
- The FindLaw snippet of § 1798.82 confirms the familiar element structure: an individual’s first name or first initial and last name in combination with specified data elements, including a Social Security number, when the name or the elements are not encrypted. — [FindLaw § 1798.82](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-82/) (retrieved November 8, 2026). The full medical-information and health-insurance sentences of the California definition were not captured in the excerpt this pass retained. Do not treat this note as quoting those California element definitions in full.
- New York: § 899-aa’s definition of private information, quoted in the SHIELD findings above, expressly includes a Social Security number and medical information (medical history, mental or physical condition, or treatment or diagnosis by a health care professional), plus health-insurance information, in the combination-and-encryption structure. — [GBL § 899-aa](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-AA).
- All 50 states, plus the District of Columbia and U.S. territories in many trackers, have breach-notification laws. That proposition is stated as background and was not re-proven by opening all 50 statutes. A practitioner map retrieved in this pass is consistent with nationwide coverage and shows health-related elements in state definitions, but it is a secondary map. — [Constangy breach map](https://www.constangy.com/data-privacy-us-ca) (retrieved November 8, 2026). Not used as a substitute for unread state texts.
- Encryption: both the California notice duty retrieved and the New York private-information definition treat encrypted data differently when the key is not also acquired. A client-side-encrypted Google Drive backup, if the key stays with the family and is not compromised, is the fact pattern those carve-outs are written for. Whether a particular backup is “encrypted” under each statute was not analyzed.

### Inferences

- Breach statutes do not require the maker to be a large or health-care business. They require a security breach of covered computerized data about a resident, plus the statute’s conduct-business or own-or-license hook.
- For the family, California’s “individual … that conducts business in California” is an awkward fit for storing one’s own household files, and New York’s notice statute is aimed at persons who own or license private information in a business setting. Household self-storage is unlikely. The moment the same data is customer data of a product sold into those states, the duties become likely.
- The open-source maker does not own residents’ SSNs or medical records merely by publishing code. If a hosted model or a support channel causes the maker to own, license, or maintain those records, the maker can have either the owner’s notice duty or the maintainer’s duty to tell the owner, depending on the state.
- Because the laws are state-by-state, a product with users in many states inherits many notice clocks, content rules, and AG notice thresholds. Cataloging them was out of scope. California’s 30-day clock and New York’s medical-information element are enough to show that both SSNs and health information are inside the breach category, not only inside the privacy category.

### Gaps

- The complete California statutory definition of “medical information” and “health insurance information” inside § 1798.82 (or the cross-referenced definition) was not fully extracted. The SSN element was.
- Whether a 2026 California amendment after the 2025 codification further changed § 1798.82 was not checked beyond the October 2025 Troutman note and the Justia 2025 text. Not verified past November 8, 2026.
- No survey of which states’ breach laws lack a “conducts business in this state” limiter, on the model of New York’s safeguard section, was done.

## 6. How the three product postures sit against thresholds

### Takeaway

The family using the device for its own household is unlikely to be the regulated entity, controller, or CCPA business under any of the statutes read, because those laws either define the household as the consumer, exclude purely personal or household processing, or apply only to for-profit businesses. The open-source maker with no customers is unlikely to be in scope unless the maker, not the family, determines the purposes of processing residents’ health data or owns or licenses their SSNs or medical information. A future consumer product is where the statutes start to bite, and they do not all wait for revenue or headcount: Washington and Nevada health laws have no minimum revenue; Connecticut’s health-data section has had no volume threshold since October 1, 2023, and from July 1, 2026 sensitive data including SSNs and passports is a no-threshold trigger; New York’s safeguard duty has a small-business standard but not an exemption; California and Maryland stay off until their numeric tests are met.

### Cited Findings

- Washington small business: fewer than 100,000 consumers’ consumer health data in a calendar year, or less than 50 percent of gross revenue from those activities and fewer than 25,000 consumers. Still regulated after June 30, 2024. No employee test. — [RCW 19.373.010(28)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010); [AG FAQ question 1](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy).
- Nevada health law: no small-business threshold found in NRS 603A.400–.550. — [NRS chapter 603A](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-603a.html).
- Connecticut: 100,000 consumers, or 25,000 plus more than 25 percent of gross revenue from sales, until June 30, 2026, for the general Act; from July 1, 2026, 35,000 consumers, or any processing of sensitive data, or any offer to sell personal data. Consumer-health provisions in § 42-526 ignore § 42-516’s numbers. No employee test. Household exclusion in § 42-524(e)(2), not repealed by the 2026 subsection substitutions retrieved. — [C.G.S. §§ 42-516, 42-524, 42-526](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_743jj.htm); [Public Act 25-113 § 6](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/act/pa/pdf/2025PA-00113-R00SB-01295-PA.pdf).
- California: for-profit, does business in California, and $26,625,000 preceding-year gross revenue (CPI figure effective January 1, 2025), or buys/sells/shares personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households, or 50 percent or more of revenue from selling or sharing. — [§ 1798.140(d)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1798-140/); [CPPA CPI page](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html).
- New York safeguards: any person or business that owns or licenses a resident’s computerized private information; scaled program if under 50 employees, or under $3 million gross annual revenue in each of the last three fiscal years, or under $5 million in assets. — [GBL § 899-bb](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-BB).
- Maryland: 35,000 consumers, or 10,000 consumers and more than 20 percent of gross revenue from selling personal data; effective October 1, 2025. — [Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/resources-info/Pages/data-privacy.aspx).

### Inferences

- Employee count matters for New York’s scaled safeguard program (under 50). It was not a threshold in the Washington, Nevada, Connecticut, California, or Maryland provisions read.
- Consumer count matters immediately for Washington only as the line between “small business” timing, which has already passed, and a larger regulated entity. It matters for Connecticut’s general Act below 35,000 only if the maker does not process sensitive data and does not sell personal data. Processing health data, SSNs, or passport numbers of Connecticut consumers removes that shelter after July 1, 2026, and the health-specific duties were already unshielded by the count. It matters for California at 100,000 consumers or households whose personal information is bought, sold, or shared, which is a narrower verb set than “processed.” It matters for Maryland at 35,000, or 10,000 if sale revenue exceeds 20 percent.
- Revenue matters for California at $26,625,000 gross, for California and the pre-2026 Connecticut law as a percentage of revenue from selling (or, in California, sharing) personal data, for Maryland as more than 20 percent from sales combined with 10,000 consumers, and for New York as the $3 million / three-year small-business line. Unknown revenue cannot be mapped to those lines. It does not, by itself, keep a Washington or Nevada health-data regulated entity, or a post-July 2026 Connecticut sensitive-data controller, outside the statute.
- Extraterritoriality is “conducts business in the state or targets products or services to residents,” plus, for Washington and Nevada health laws, a consumer whose data is collected in the state even if not a resident. Merely locating a server, or merely publishing source code that a resident could download, is not what the Washington AG described as enough. Offering the product to households in the state is the fact that makes (c) likely or depends rather than unlikely.
- The confidential-computing check and the encrypted Drive backup do not create a statutory exemption in any law read. They are relevant to whether the maker, rather than the family, determines purposes and means, whether a cloud operator is a processor, and whether a later breach involves readable personal information and a key.
